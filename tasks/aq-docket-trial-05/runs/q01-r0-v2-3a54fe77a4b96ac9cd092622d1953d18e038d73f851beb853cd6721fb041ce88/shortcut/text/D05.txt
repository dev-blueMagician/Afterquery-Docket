A. FOURTH AMENDMENT CLAIMS

The Fourth Amendment provides in relevant part that “[t]he right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated.” U.S. CONST. AMEND. IV. The cursory allegations in the Complaint, quoted in full above, may fit the description of “short and plain,” but they do not provide sufficient details to satisfy the further requirement in Rule 8 that the short and plain statement “show[ ] that [Plaintiff] is entitled to relief.” FED. R. CIV. P. 8(a)(2). The Complaint’s allegations state only that Alcon trespassed on Plaintiff’s property, without any facts to indicate that, in doing so, Alcon subjected Plaintiff to a search or seizure of her person, house, papers, or effects. Indeed, the above-quoted allegations may not even be sufficient to show that a trespass occurred. The Court of Appeals of New Mexico has defined trespass as a physical invasion of property that directly infringes on another’s right of possession. Dolan v. Fed. Emergency Mgmt. Agency, 760 F. Supp. 3d 1200, 1247 (D.N.M. 2024) (citing Padilla v. Lawrence, 1984-NMCA-064, ¶ 26, 101 N.M. 556, 563, 685 P.2d 964, 971); see also RESTATEMENT (SECOND) OF TORTS § 158 (1965) (“One is subject to liability to another for trespass, irrespective of whether he thereby causes harm to any legally protected interest of the other, if he intentionally ... enters land in the possession of the other”).

The 2021 trespass allegedly occurred on “Geneva Langworthy’s property” located at 12 1/2 Towhee Lane. But the 2022 trespass allegedly occurred “on Jaybird Lane,” and, unlike 12 1/2 Towhee Lane, the Complaint does not allege that Jaybird Lane is “Geneva Langworthy’s

B. EQUAL PROTECTION CLAIM

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment provides, “No State shall ...

deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. CONST. AMEND.

XIV, § 1. Generally speaking, “[t]he Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment ... is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” City of Cleburne

v. Cleburne Living Ctr., 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985). Thus, “[t]he Equal Protection Clause provides a basis ... for contending that general rules are being applied in an arbitrary or discriminatory way.”

Jones v. Helms, 452 U.S. 412, 423–24 (1981). “To survive a motion to dismiss, plaintiffs raising these claims must allege ‘intentional unlawful discrimination or allege facts that are at least Case 1:24-cv-01291-MLG-KRS Document 64 Filed 09/18/26 Page 8 of 17

9 susceptible of an inference of discriminatory intent’ in their complaints.” Clark v. Washington State Dep’t of Health, 735 F. Supp. 3d 1334, 1347 (W.D. Wash. 2024) (quoting Monteiro v. Tempe Union High Sch. Dist., 158 F.3d 1022, 1026 (9th Cir. 1998)).

An equal protection claim can be established in one of two ways: a “class-based” theory or a “class-of-one” theory. A “class-based”

theory alleges that the defendant discriminated against the plaintiff on the basis of her membership in an “identifiable class,” while a “class-of-one” theory alleges that the plaintiff has been intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated and that there is no rational basis for the difference in treatment.

Id. (internal quotation marks and citations omitted).

Plaintiff alleges that Defendant Alcon violated her right to equal protection because he investigated allegations by her neighbor Todd Tatum of trespass by her onto Tatum’s property, but did nothing when she reported Todd Tatum’s trespass onto her property. Plaintiff’s allegations do not identify any class-based discrimination as a motivating factor for the alleged discriminatory treatment, and therefore she appears to be trying to allege a “class of one” claim. To adequately allege such a claim, Plaintiff must allege facts showing that she was treated differently from others similarly situated and that there is no rational basis for the difference in treatment. McGuire v.

Nielsen, 448 F. Supp. 3d 1213, 1253 (D.N.M. 2020); see Village of Willowbrook v. Olech, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000) (concluding that, to prevail on a “class-of-one” claim, a “plaintiff must show he or she (as opposed to a class in which he is a member) was ‘intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated’”).

In the years after the Supreme Court’s decision in Village of Willowbrook v. Olech, the Tenth Circuit expressed strong reservations about entertaining class-of-one challenges:

[T]he concept of a class-of-one equal protection claim could effectively provide a federal cause of action for review of almost every executive and administrative decision made by state actors. It is always possible for persons aggrieved by government action to allege, and

Case 1:24-cv-01291-MLG-KRS Document 64 Filed 09/18/26 Page 9 of 17

10 almost always possible to produce evidence, that they were treated differently from others, with regard to everything from zoning to licensing to speeding to tax evaluation. It would become the task of federal courts and juries, then, to inquire into the grounds for differential treatment and to decide whether those grounds were sufficiently reasonable to satisfy equal protection review. This would constitute the federal courts as general-purpose second-guessers of the reasonableness of broad areas of state and local decisionmaking: a role that is both ill-suited to the federal courts and offensive to state and local autonomy in our federal system.

Jennings v. City of Stillwater, 383 F.3d [1199,] at 1210-11 [(10th Cir. 2004)]. See Kan. Penn Gaming, LLC v. Collins, 656 F.3d [1210,] at 1216-17 [10th Cir. 2011)] (“The latitude afforded police officers, IRS agents, university administrators, zoning officials, and other, similar government actors necessarily results in a sizeable amount of random variation in outcome. If even innocuous inconsistencies gave rise to equal protection litigation, government action would be paralyzed.”). “It is therefore imperative for the class-of-one plaintiff to provide a specific and detailed account of the nature of the preferred treatment of the favored class.” Jennings

v. City of Stillwater, 383 F.3d at 1214.

McGuire, 448 F. Supp. 3d at 1253 (emphasis added).

With these legal principles in mind, it is clear that Plaintiff’s allegations lack sufficient factual content to be able to say that she has plausibly alleged an equal protection claim. First, there is the question of standing. See Doe v. Anoka Cnty., No. 25-1568, 2026 WL 2293287, at *3 (8th Cir. Aug. 10, 2026) (holding that crime victims lack standing to assert equal protection claims against prosecutors and law enforcement officials for alleged failures to investigate (citing, inter alia, Pratt v. Helms, 73 F4th 592, 595 (8th Cir. 2023) (holding that the plaintiff lacked standing to bring § 1983 claims against officials from the Camden County Sheriff’s Department for their alleged failure to investigate an assault against him, concluding that “a victim has no constitutional right to an investigation of a crime committed against him—at least where ... the victim brings a class-of-one equal protection claim”)); see also Linda R.S. v. Richard D., 410 U.S. 614, 619 (1973) Case 1:24-cv-01291-MLG-KRS Document 64 Filed 09/18/26 Page 10 of 17

11

(holding that a private citizen lacks a judicially cognizable interest in the prosecution or nonprosecution of another and therefore lacks standing to sue an official for his alleged failure to prosecute an alleged criminal).

Second, Plaintiff’s factual allegations suggest in only the barest of terms that she was similarly situated to Todd Tatum. See Selsor v. Workman, 644 F.3d 984, 1016 (10th Cir. 2011) (stating that “the degree to which others are viewed as similarly situated [for equal protection analysis purposes] depends substantially on the facts and context of the case, and that, consequently, many legitimate variables may serve to distinguish the plaintiff from those other persons”). In fact, even the truncated facts Plaintiff includes in the Complaint suggest a significant difference between her trespass complaint against Tatum and Tatum’s trespass complaint against her: Alcon told Plaintiff that “he didn’t know whether the road [Jaybird Lane, where Tatum’s trespass allegedly occurred] was private.” (Doc. 1-2 at 3). Plaintiff alleges Alcon’s statement showed “gross incompetence.” (Id.). But gross incompetence is not the same thing as intentional discrimination, which is what is required to state a valid equal protection claim. In any event, [t]he necessary factor making” a class-of-one equal protection claim viable is “the existence of a clear standard against which departures, even for a single plaintiff, c[a]n be readily assessed.” McGuire,

448 F. Supp. 3d at 1254 (quoting Engquist v. Or. Dep't of Agric., 553 U.S. 591, 602 (2008)). An officer’s duty to investigate reports of trespass is not subject to a clear standard against which departures can be easily assessed. See Flowers v. City of Minneapolis, Minn., 558 F.3d 794, 799-

800 (8th Cir. 2009) (pointing out that the Supreme Court in Engquist used law enforcement officers to illustrate forms of state action that by their nature involve discretionary decisionmaking). Thus, as was held in Flowers, one could conclude that a police officer’s investigative decisions simply “may not be attacked in a class-of-one equal protection claim.” Id. at 800.

Third, although a police officer’s investigative decisionmay “remain subject to traditional class-based equal protection analysis,” id., such a claim would require a discriminatory or impermissible class-based motive by the officer in deciding whether, when, and how to investigate a crime. See, e.g., id. at 798 (citing St. German of Alaska E. Orthodox Catholic Church v. United States, 840 F.2d 1087, 1095 (2d Cir. 1988)). And currently Plaintiff has not alleged a sufficient claim under this framework.That is, she does not allege she is a member of a suspect class, or that a fundamental right is implicated, nor has she alleged any facts to suggest that the differing treatment she allegedly received was motivated by a class-based discriminatory purpose. See Village of Willowbrook, 528 U.S. at 564. Accordingly, as alleged, Plaintiff’s equal protection claim must be dismissed.

C. INSTRUCTIONS FOR FILING An AMENDED COMPLAINT

If Plaintiff asserts the Court should not dismiss her Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment claims against Defendant Alcon, she must file an amended complaint that complies with the Federal and Local Rules of Civil Procedure, including, as discussed above, the requirement of a “short and plain statement” of her claims “showing that [she] is entitled to relief.” In addition, Plaintiff must refrain from adding any new parties or claims to any amended complaint she files in response to this Order To Show Cause. This is because the issue of whether Plaintiff is entitled to amend the Complaint to add new parties and/or new claims cannot be addressed until after Plaintiff demonstrates that the current Complaint is not subject to dismissal. In the event Plaintiff’s Complaint is not dismissed, Plaintiff will have the opportunity to present argument that she should be allowed to add claims and/or parties to this case through a later filed motion in which she seeks leave of Court to file a second amended complaint.

In short, the purpose of this Order To Show Cause is to address deficiencies in Plaintiff’s current claims by requiring Plaintiff to file an amendment to those claims only. This Order does not grant Plaintiff leave to amend the Complaint for any other purpose, such as to add new claims or parties. Before Plaintiff may file an amended complaint that adds new claims or parties, Plaintiff must first file an amended complaint in response to this Order To Show Cause. The Court then must find that the amended complaint filed in response to this Order To Show Cause adequately pleads a claim for relief against Defendant Alcon. If the Court finds that it does, then Plaintiff may file a motion pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a)(2) seeking leave of court to file a second amended complaint, which motion must comply with the Federal and Local Rules of Civil Procedure and the Court’s previous orders. Plaintiff’s motion for leave to amend to add new claims and parties will then be addressed by the undersigned through an order setting forth proposed findings and recommended disposition on the motion, which will then be addressed by the presiding judge. For the undersigned to recommend permitting such an amended filing, Plaintiff must present legal and factual arguments in her motion seeking leave to amend for why the new claims she seeks to assert are appropriate under Rules 18 and 20, and are not barred by the restrictions imposed in Senior District Judge Johnson’s Order Imposing Filing Restrictions.
