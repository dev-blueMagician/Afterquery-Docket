## Question

UK fake-review guidance gives examples of traders commissioning reviews by offering customers or other consumers something. As FTC staff read the US consumer reviews rule, which of those examples, if any, would breach its ban on incentives tied to a review's sentiment? List them in any order.

## Answer

Free or discounted product for a five-star review, social media post offering free products for five-star reviews, reimbursing purchases once a positive review is submitted

## Components

- c1: Free or discounted product for a five-star review
- c2: social media post offering free products for five-star reviews
- c3: reimbursing purchases once a positive review is submitted

## Reasoning

Step 1 (Doc 5): The CMA's list "Commissioning fake reviews or concealed incentivised reviews" gives these examples in which a trader offers customers or consumers something: a free or discounted product in exchange for a five-star review; asking a customer to review a free or discounted product without making clear that the incentive must be disclosed; a social media post asking for five-star reviews in exchange for free products; a refund or gift card if a customer changes a negative review to remove the negative commentary; reimbursement of purchases once a positive review is submitted. The list's last example, buying reviews generated by software applications, offers no consumer anything.

Step 2 (Doc 2): FTC staff say the rule "does not prohibit giving incentives for reviews, as long as there isn’t an express or implied requirement that the reviews have to express a particular sentiment"; paying incentives for 5-star reviews "would violate Section 465.4" even with a disclosure; and the rule does not prohibit "offering compensation to a consumer to remove or change a review", which may instead violate the FTC Act.

Step 3: Applying Step 2 to Step 1, the five-star product offer, the five-star social media post and the reimbursement for a positive review are tied to a review's sentiment and breach Section 465.4. The undisclosed free-product request sets no sentiment requirement, and staff place the refund for changing a negative review outside the rule.

Decoy (Doc 2): read literally, the Section 465.4 ban on incentives tied to a review's sentiment also seems to catch the refund offer, giving a fourth item; staff's answer that the rule does not prohibit offering compensation to remove or change a review excludes it.

## Targeted failure modes

### Failure mode 1
Trap: Linking across documents
How a careless solver fails: Misapplies a rule or premise
Where the trap sits: Doc 5, the UK fake-review guidance, lists among its examples of commissioning reviews "Contacting a
customer who has left a negative review and offering them a refund and/or a gift card if they change their review to
remove the negative commentary (so that it is no longer reflective of their genuine experience)." Doc 2, FTC staff's
answers on the US consumer reviews rule, states the ban the question names: the rule "does not prohibit giving
incentives for reviews, as long as there isn’t an express or implied requirement that the reviews have to express a
particular sentiment", and paying incentives for 5-star reviews "would violate Section 465.4". A refund offered only if
the customer removes the negative commentary looks like exactly such an incentive tied to a review's sentiment.
The wrong answer a careless solver gives: Free or discounted product for a five-star review, social media post offering
free products for five-star reviews, refund or gift card for changing a negative review, reimbursing purchases once a
positive review is submitted. The solver applies the general sentiment test to each example, treats the refund for
removing negative commentary as an incentive conditioned on the review's sentiment, and lists four examples instead of
three.
Why the correct answer avoids it: A careful reader checks whether staff address that exact conduct. In the same section
on Section 465.4, Doc 2 asks "Does the rule prohibit my business from offering compensation to a consumer to remove or
change a review?" and answers "No, but paying consumers to change or remove truthful negative reviews may violate the
FTC Act as an unfair or deceptive act or practice". So, as FTC staff read the rule, the refund for changing a negative
review falls outside it; it may breach the FTC Act instead, which the question does not ask about. The five-star
product offer, the five-star social media post and the reimbursement for a positive review remain. Each is an incentive
expressly conditioned on a five-star or positive review: staff say paying incentives for 5-star reviews "would violate
Section 465.4", and that "The rule prohibits businesses from implying that a review has to be positive in order for
consumers to get an incentive for the review."
