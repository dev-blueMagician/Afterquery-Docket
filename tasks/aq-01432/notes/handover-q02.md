### Step-by-step reasoning

```text
Step 1 (Doc 5): The CMA's list "Commissioning fake reviews or concealed incentivised reviews" gives these examples in which a trader offers customers or consumers something: a free or discounted product in exchange for a five-star review; asking a customer to review a free or discounted product without making clear that the incentive must be disclosed; a social media post asking for five-star reviews in exchange for free products; a refund or gift card if a customer changes a negative review to remove the negative commentary; reimbursement of purchases once a positive review is submitted. The list's last example, buying reviews generated by software applications, offers no consumer anything.

Step 2 (Doc 2): FTC staff say the rule "does not prohibit giving incentives for reviews, as long as there isn’t an express or implied requirement that the reviews have to express a particular sentiment"; paying incentives for 5-star reviews "would violate Section 465.4" even with a disclosure; and the rule does not prohibit "offering compensation to a consumer to remove or change a review", which may instead violate the FTC Act.

Step 3: Applying Step 2 to Step 1, the five-star product offer, the five-star social media post and the reimbursement for a positive review are tied to a review's sentiment and breach Section 465.4. The undisclosed free-product request sets no sentiment requirement, and staff place the refund for changing a negative review outside the rule.

Decoy (Doc 2): read literally, the Section 465.4 ban on incentives tied to a review's sentiment also seems to catch the refund offer, giving a fourth item; staff's answer that the rule does not prohibit offering compensation to remove or change a review excludes it.
```

Why the correct answer avoids it:

```text
A careful reader checks whether staff address that exact conduct. In the same section on Section 465.4, Doc 2 asks "Does the rule prohibit my business from offering compensation to a consumer to remove or change a review?" and answers "No, but paying consumers to change or remove truthful negative reviews may violate the FTC Act as an unfair or deceptive act or practice". So, as FTC staff read the rule, the refund for changing a negative review falls outside it; it may breach the FTC Act instead, which the question does not ask about. The five-star product offer, the five-star social media post and the reimbursement for a positive review remain. Each is an incentive expressly conditioned on a five-star or positive review: staff say paying incentives for 5-star reviews "would violate Section 465.4", and that "The rule prohibits businesses from implying that a review has to be positive in order for consumers to get an incentive for the review."
```
