The Fiscal and Distributional Impact of the Reform of Unemployment Benefits in Belgium 

Kristine Van Herck, Savina Princen, Hannes Serruys and Arne Depoortere 

Abstract  
The employment rate in Belgium remains low, partly reflecting features of the tax and benefit system that weaken work incentives. More specifically, the benefit system in Belgium provided – until 1 January 2026 – unemployment  benefits  of  unlimited  duration,  which  made  Belgium  the  only  EU  Member  State  in  which unemployment  benefits  were  not  limited  in  time.  As  this  feature  may  have  discouraged  benefit  recipients from  taking  up  work,  the  federal  government  adopted  in  2025  a  long-awaited  reform  introducing  a maximum duration for unemployment benefits. Unemployment benefit recipients affected by the reform are expected  to  re–enter  employment,  while  the  most  vulnerable  long–term  unemployed  people  can  rely  on minimum income support.  
Based  on  micro-simulations  using  the  EUROMOD  model,  this  economic  brief  analyses  the  budgetary  and distributional effects of the unemployment benefit reform under different labour supply assumptions, taking into account the interaction  with the  minimum income support system. The analysis  finds that, even in the absence of labour supply effects, the reform is expected to generate net budgetary savings. However, when potential labour supply effects are taken into account, the net budgetary savings are significantly higher (up to  EUR  2.1  billion).  The  overall  impact  on  poverty  and  inequality  is  expected  to  be  limited  due  to  the cushioning effect of the minimum income support system and the potential increase in employment.

## INTRODUCTION

Employment is comparatively low in Belgium, partially due to financial disincentives to work. 
The employment rate was 72.8% in 2025 (vs 76.1% on average in the EU), well below the 2030 national target  of  80%.  Low  employment  is  mainly  driven  by  a  low  employment  rate  among  disadvantaged groups, including people with low educational attainment, with a migrant background, with disabilities, or aged  over  60.  A  comparison  with  other  EU  Member  States  suggests  that  low  employment  can  be partially  explained  by  financial  disincentives  to  work.  Belgium  levies  high  labour  taxes  (social contributions  and  income  taxes)  compared  to  the  EU  average.  Moreover,  the  design  of  the  tax  and benefit system is complex and discourages recipients from taking up work or working more hours.1 To  help  address  this  labour  market  challenge,  the  federal  government  adopted  in  2025  a reform  of  the  unemployment  benefit  system.2 This  reform  limits  –  as  a  general  rule  ––  the maximum entitlement to unemployment benefits, which were unlimited in time until 1 January 2026, to maximum  two  years.  In  addition,  the  reform  limits  activation  benefits  for  young  people  who  have completed or ceased their studies (allocation d’insertion / inschakelingsuitkering) from three to one year. 
The reform of the unemployment benefit system is being gradually implemented since 1 January 2026. 
The  reform  aims  at  encouraging  unemployed  people  to  reintegrate  faster  in  the  labour market, although some of them are expected to rely on minimum income support. The federal government  expects  the  reform  to  restore  the  balance  between  social  contributions  and  benefits  to ensure  the  long–term  sustainability  of  the  social  security  system,  and  to  generate  some  budgetary savings. It also expects the reform to spur unemployment benefit recipients to take up work again and to permanently alter the labour market dynamics. It aims at “fundamentally reforming the unemployment benefit  system  into  a  genuine  insurance  system  […],  maximum  filling  open  vacancies,  increasing  the employment rate towards 80 % by 2029”3. The most vulnerable long–term unemployed people, however, are  expected  to  fall  back  on  the  minimum  income  support  system.  This  is  expected  to  reduce  the potential cost savings of the reform, but also to minimise its impact on poverty and inequality. 
This  economic  brief  aims  to  analyse  the  budgetary  and  distributional  effects  of  the  key feature  of  the  reform,  namely  limiting  unemployment  benefits  in  time.  In  the  analysis,  the interplay with other features of the benefit system, namely the uptake of minimum income support, will be studied based on micro-simulation using the EUROMOD model.4 In addition, also several scenarios as regards the impact of the reform on labour supply will be considered. The analysis is static in nature and therefore captures only the immediate “morning-after” effects of the reform, without taking into account longer-term behavioural responses beyond the labour supply scenarios considered. The note is organised as  follows.  The  first  section  describes  the  current  and  reformed  unemployment  benefit  system  in Belgium.  The  second  section  analyses  budgetary  and  distributional  effects  of  the  reform  based  on EUROMOD simulations, considering also potential labour supply effects. The final section concludes.

THE UNEMPLOYMENT BENEFIT SYSTEM 
ROLE AND DESIGN OF UNEMPLOYMENT BENEFITS  

Unemployment  benefits  protect  workers  from  income  loss,  support  job  search,  and  act  as automatic stabilisers during economic downturns. The main purpose of unemployment benefits is to provide income replacement in case of unemployment to avoid a significant deterioration in the living standards of unemployed people. By insuring workers against the risks linked to job loss, unemployment benefits provide a safety net for individuals and households. In addition, unemployment benefits operate as an automatic stabiliser within the macroeconomic framework: during economic downturns, increased benefit  payments  help  sustain  aggregate  demand,  while  in  periods  of  economic  expansion,  reduced reliance on unemployment benefits moderates public expenditure.  
The  duration  of  unemployment  benefits  plays  a  pivotal  role  in  balancing  social  protection with  labour  market  incentives.  Short  benefit  durations  can  limit  long-term  dependency  and encourage a rapid return to work, but they risk exposing individuals to income insecurity and poor job-matching, especially in economic downturns. Conversely, more generous durations provide unemployed workers with sufficient time to search for suitable employment and prevent poverty.5 Yet, long duration benefits can have a negative impact on labour supply. In the presence of relatively loose job search and availability  conditions  like  in  Belgium  (OECD,  2023),  incentive  problems  associated  with  moral  hazard may  lead  to  sub–optimal  job  search  intensity.,  This  may  result  in  longer  unemployment  spells,  which further  reduce  the  employability  of  jobseekers  (e.g.  Mortensen,  1997;  Shavell  and  Weiss,  1979). 
Furthermore,  unemployment  benefits  with  a  longer  duration  entail  a  significant  budgetary  cost  and increase  the  administrative  complexity  of  monitoring  compliance  with  job  search  and  availability requirements, as these need to be monitored over a longer period (e.g. Salvatori, 2022). 
In addition to the duration, also the time profile of the unemployment benefits is a relevant policy  parameter  for  the  design  of  the  benefit  scheme. In  general,  unemployment  benefits  are degressive  benefits,  meaning  that  the  income  replacement  rates  decline  over  time.  This  allows  for  a front-loaded  provision  of  income  support  at  the  start  of  the  unemployment  spell,  while  gradually reducing  the  relative  generosity  to  encourage  job  search  and  re-employment  (Cahuc  et  al.,  2014). 
However,  the  merits  of  this  design  have  been  contested  arguing  that  long-term  unemployed  people respond less to changes in benefit levels than short-term unemployed people as their chances of finding a  job  decline  with  the  duration  of  their  unemployment  spell,  irrespective  of  their  search  effort (Spinnewijn, 2015; Kolsrud et al., 2018; Campos et al., 2022). This suggests that degressivity should be more prominently deployed in the early stages of the unemployment spell instead of gradually declining over the spell.  
Finally, active search requirements and conditionality are also relevant to the incentives of jobseekers.  Empirical  research  demonstrates  that  mandatory  reporting  of  job  search  activities, participation  in  activation  programs,  and  proportionate  sanctions  for  non–compliance  significantly enhance re-employment outcomes (Boone et al., 2007). Integrating search requirements with degressive benefits  ensures  that  the  financial  incentive  created  by  declining  replacement  rates  reinforce  the jobseekers’ search behaviour. Furthermore, coupling these instruments with activation measures, such as job  counselling  and  training,  helps  mitigate  the  risk  of  long-term  unemployment,  particularly  for vulnerable groups.

THE 2026 REFORM OF THE UNEMPLOYMENT BENEFIT SYSTEM IN BELGIUM 

The  federal  government  significantly  reformed  the  unemployment  benefits,  limiting  their duration and reinforcing the link with the jobseeker’s employment history. The reform is being gradually  implemented  from  1  January  2026  onwards  and  limits  the  maximum  duration  of unemployment benefits to two years. Moreover, it changed the eligibility criteria by making the required employment history independent of the age of the person, except for some older workers (see below). 
The  reform  reinforced  the  link  between  the  duration  of  benefits  and  the  employment  history  of  the recipient:  when  a  person  has  worked  for  one  year  in  the  past  three  years,  the  person  is  entitled  to unemployment  benefits  for  one  year.  For  every  four  additional  months  of  work,  an  extra  month  of benefits is granted. Hence, a person who has worked five years is entitled to the full two–year benefit period. Also, activation benefits for young people who have completed or ceased their studies (allocation d’insertion / inschakelingsuitkering) were reduced in time from three to one year.11 

11 In addition, the reform reduced the mandatory period that a school-leaver must wait before becoming eligible for the unemployment benefit from approximately one year (310 days) to 6 months (156 days).

Some unemployed  people  are exempted  from  this  limitation  in  time. Older workers (individuals aged 55 and over), who worked at least 30 years (with a minimum of 156 days per year) are exempted from the time limitation. This threshold will increase to 35 years by 2030, tightening access over time. 
Furthermore,  some  sectoral  exceptions  apply  for  artists,  dock  workers,  and  sea  fishers  due  to  the irregular  nature  of  their  employment.  Moreover,  jobseekers  taking  a  training  course  for  critical  health care functions will continue to benefit from unemployment support beyond the two years’ duration. As a transitional measure, also unemployed people who started a training for a shortage profession in 2025 remain  eligible  for  unemployment  benefits  during  their  training,  even  when  their  total  unemployment spell exceeds two years. 

SPILLOVERS BETWEEN THE UNEMPLOYMENT REFORM AND THE MINIMUM INCOME  

Some  unemployed  individuals  who  lose  their  entitlement  to  unemployment  benefits  may qualify for minimum income support provided they meet a number of eligibility requirements. 
Eligibility  criteria  for  income  support  (leefloon  /  revenu  d’intégration  sociale)  relate  to  household composition,  age,  nationality,  residence,  lack  of  financial  resources,  willingness  to  work  and  having exhausted  rights  to  other  benefits.  The  gross  monthly  amount  of  minimum  income  (not  subject  to taxation  –  on  the  date  of  1  March  2026) is  EUR 893,67  per person for  eligible  co–habiting  household members,  EUR  1.340,47  for  a  single  person  and  EUR  1.811,57  for  a  person  with  a  dependent  family. 
These amounts may be combined with some labour income, with specific family benefits and tax credits, and with  other benefits  such  as  for  housing,  heating  and  social  assistance. By  limiting  the  duration  of the  unemployment  benefits  in  time,  some  unemployed  individuals  who  are  no  longer  eligible  for  an unemployment benefit are expected to turn to minimum income support as a fallback option.  
In principle, the reform may also generate spillovers to the disability scheme, as individuals with  health  limitations  who  experience  difficulties  in  meeting  the  availability  requirements may increasingly turn to disability benefits. However, eligibility for disability benefits is subject to a formal medical assessment, which limits the scope for additional inflows from unemployment. Moreover, since disability benefits are in most cases more generous than unemployment benefits, individuals who clearly qualify for disability support are likely to have already applied. Therefore, spillover effects with the disability scheme are not included in this economic brief. 

BUDGETARY AND DISTRIBUTIONAL EFFECTS OF THE 
UNEMPLOYMENT BENEFIT REFORM 
The  budgetary  and  distributional  effect  of  the  unemployment  benefit  reform  are  simulated using the EUROMOD model (Sutherland and Figari, 2013). The EUROMOD model allows to analyse the effects  of  tax  and  social  benefit  policy  reforms  on  household  incomes  across  EU  Member  States.  The model operates by applying country-specific tax and benefit rules to anonymised household data from the EU Statistics on Income and Living Conditions (EU-SILC).12 This allows to assess the budgetary and distributional  effects  of  reforms  as  compared  to  a baseline  scenario.  The reform  simulation  limits  the duration of unemployment benefits to two years for people aged 55 and below as well as those older than 55 years with an employment history of less than 30 years.13 (see Box 1 for more details on the modelling assumptions).

Four alternative labour supply scenarios are modelled through the EUROMOD Labour Market Adjustment  (LMA)  add-on. When losing unemployment benefits, part of those unemployed for more than two years will take up employment and for these workers a counterfactual wage will be set based on their individual and household characteristics (see Box 1). In addition, more recent unemployed (with an unemployment duration of less than two years) may also be motivated to take up employment faster when  they  face  the  prospect  of  losing  their  unemployment  benefit  after  two  years.  To  capture  these labour  supply  responses,  the  EUROMOD  Labour  Market  Adjustment  (LMA)  add-on  is  used  to  model transitions  in  employment  status.  The  LMA  add-on  implements  labour  supply  by  reassigning  labour market status for the relevant share of affected individuals and imputing associated earnings, months of  work,  and  hours  under  standardised  assumptions  (e.g.  wage  employment  rather  than  self-employment)  (see  Box  1).  Other  income  sources  tied  to  previous  labour  market  status  are  adjusted accordingly, and eligibility for benefits is recalculated. There are four alternative scenarios modelled for the labour supply effects, assuming an uptake of employment by, respectively, 10%, 20% and 33% of those that lose their unemployment benefit following the reform. The first two scenarios are selected ad hoc,  while  the  last  one  (33%) is  in  line  with  the  estimates  by  the  federal  government  that  one  out  of three individuals losing unemployment benefit entitlement is assumed to take up employment again. 
The  simulated  budgetary  and  distributional  effects  of  the  reform  also  reflect  the  interplay with  minimum  income  support.  Following  the  reform,  some  beneficiaries  who  lose  unemployment benefits  will  become  eligible  for  minimum  income  support.  However,  for  this  type  of  support  a beneficiary  needs  to  apply  as  it  is  not  granted  automatically.  As  the  EUROMOD  baseline  simulations assume full benefit take-up, they tend to overestimate the number of beneficiaries of minimum income. 
To account for this bias, the standard EUROMOD model for Belgium uses a ‘benefit take-up adjustment’ of minimum income support. In the description of the results, both simulations (full-take up and adjusted for  non-take  up)  are  presented  to  provide  an  upper  and  lower  bound  for  the  minimum  income simulation. 

BOX – EUROMOD MICRO-SIMULATIONS OF THE UNEMPLOYMENT BENEFIT REFORM 

Micro-simulation models  such as  EUROMOD allow to take into account the interactions between the reform element (limiting the unemployment benefits in time) and all other features of the tax and  benefit system. 
These interactions might have a non-trivial impact on work incentives, especially in countries such as Belgium with  well-developed  social  systems  (see  HOME  |  EUROMOD  -  Tax-benefit  microsimulation  model  for  the European Union for more details on the model). 
The simulation of limiting unemployment benefits in time is carried out in several steps: 

  Adjust EUROMOD input data so that unemployment benefits are set to zero if the person has been unemployed  for  24  months  or  longer  and  is  aged  55  years  or  below.  In  addition,  unemployment benefits are also set equal to zero for people older than 55 years if they have an employment history of less than 30 years.  

  Run  EUROMOD  with  the  adjusted  data.  In  this  simulation,  a  person  whose  unemployment  benefits have  been set to zero  might be assigned certain means-tested benefits for  which  he  would  not be eligible  while  receiving  unemployment  benefits,  depending  on  the  eligibility  rules  modelled  in EUROMOD’s  baseline.  In  this  way,  the  simulation  accounts  for  the  interaction  of  all  the  tax-benefit components,  including  the  indirect  effect  this  reform  has  on  social  insurance  contributions  and personal  income  taxes.  However,  due  to  data  limitations,  it  is  not  possible  to  model  all  eligibility criteria for the minimum income benefits. Eligibility for the minimum income benefit depends on both income and wealth of the individual (and of other members of the family or household). Since wealth is not reported in SILC data, the EUROMOD simulation only considers an estimate of wealth based on reported  income  from  assets.  Further,  the  EUROMOD  simulation  does  not  distinguish  between  the

  Model  the  labour  supply  effect  (transitions  in  employment  status)  through  the  EUROMOD  Labour Market  Adjustment  (LMA)  add-on.  The  method  first  identifies  who  is  affected  by  the  reform. 
Subsequently,  a  sorting  by  alignment  method  is  used  to  identify  who  is  most  likely  to  switch  to employment.  This  method  uses  a  logistic  regression  model  to  predict  the  probability  of  being employed  based  on  socio-demographic  characteristics.  Then,  the  affected  individuals  are  sorted based  on  the  employment  probability  resulting  from  the  logistic  regression  model.  Accounting  for population  weights,  the  method  allows  to  identify  the  first  X%  of  individuals  who  would  switch  to employment  in  the  counterfactual  simulation.  The  LMA-addon  of  EUROMOD  will  switch  the employment  status  of  these  individuals  to  full-time  employment.  The  counterfactual  gross  hourly wage is part of the standard EUROMOD input data and calculated using a Heckman selection model. 

Following  the  unemployment  benefit  reform,  some  beneficiaries  who  lose  unemployment  benefits  will become eligible for minimum income support. While not all eligible individuals will request minimum income support, EUROMOD baseline simulations assume full benefit take-up (see EUROMOD country report provides more information on eligibility rules and EUROMOD take-up adjustment assumptions). To correct for this bias, the standard EUROMOD model for Belgium uses a ‘benefit take-up adjustment’ of minimum income support. 
In particular, the Belgian EUROMOD model adjusts for non-take-up with a random non-take-up correction by applying  the  take-up  proportion  as  estimated  by  the  TAKE  project  (https://www.belspo.be/belspo/brain-be/projects/FinalReports/TAKE_FinRep.pdf). 
The  default  benefit  take-up  adjustment  (BTA)  in  EUROMOD  uses  official  statistics  for  the  calibration  of random  assignments  of  minimum  income.  The  number  of  total  minimum  income  recipients  in  the  country simulated  without  take-up  adjustment  is  compared  with  the  actual  number  of  minimum  income  recipients based  on  official  statistics.  When  BTA  is  “on”,  the  simulation  of  minimum  income  is,  therefore,  somewhat anchored to the take-up of minimum income in the baseline policy scenario, which is 56% in this simulation. 
However,  removing  unemployment  benefits  for  the  long-term  unemployed  results  in  a  deviation  from  the baseline scenario because the number of minimum income recipients is increasing, which will likely affect the take-up probabilities. For this reason, the note presents the results of both simulations (BTA turned “off” and BTA turned “on”).  

## BUDGETARY EFFECT

Disregarding  labour  supply  effects,  the  unemployment  benefit  reform  leads  to  a  positive budgetary impact on public finances. The first three rows of Table 1 show the budgetary effects of limiting  unemployment  benefits  to  a  maximum  of  two  years,  both  without  and  with  adjustments  for take-up  of  minimum  income  support,  in  the  absence  of  labour  supply  effects.  Simulations  show  that reducing  unemployment  spending  annually  increases  the  net  budgetary  position  of  the  government between  EUR  355.4  million  (lower  bound,  when  full  take-up  of  minimum  income  support  is  assumed) and  EUR  838.9  million  (upper  bound,  with  take-up  adjustments),  reflecting  direct  taxes  and  social contributions  net  of  cash  transfers.  The  decrease  in  unemployment  benefit  expenditure  is  substantial (EUR 1 334 million) and more than compensates for the increase in minimum income support (EUR 890 million  assuming  full  take-up  and  EUR  407  million  with  adjusted  take-up).  The  decrease  in unemployment  benefit  expenditure  also  compensates  for  the  decrease  in  direct  tax  revenues  (EUR  71 million) when labour supply effects are not considered. This tax revenue loss is explained by the fact that unemployment benefits are subject to personal income taxes, while minimum income support is not.

## DISTRIBUTIONAL EFFECTS

In  the  scenario  without  labour  supply  effects,  limiting  unemployment  benefits  in  time  has only  a  small  negative  impact  on  household  incomes  as  the  adverse  income  effect  of  the reform  is  largely  offset  by  minimum  income  support.  In  this  scenario,  disposable  household incomes  decrease  across  entire  population  on  average  by  0.32%  with  respect  to  the  baseline.  The decline is strongest for the three lowest income deciles, ranging from –3.1% in the first income decile to –0.7% in the third income decile. The impact on the higher income deciles is even smaller (Graph 5).

## CONCLUSIONS

In July 2025, the Belgian federal government adopted a reform of the unemployment benefit system  to  respond  to  the  labour  market  challenges  that  the  country  is  facing.  The  reform gradually  implemented  since  1  January  2026  consists  of  several  measures  of  which  the  main  one  is limiting  unemployment  benefits  to  two  years  for  those  younger  than  55  years  or  those  with  an employment history of less than 30 years. Some stakeholders have argued that the budgetary savings may be limited, as jobseekers who loose eligibility for unemployment support rely on minimum income support.  In  addition,  they  argued  that  the  reform  would  have  a  strong  negative  impact  on  poverty.  To assess these concerns this economic brief analyses, based on EUROMOD microsimulation, the budgetary and  distributional  effects  of  limiting  unemployment  benefits  in  time,  while  taking  into  account  the interaction with the minimum income support system. 
According  to  EUROMOD  simulations,  the  budgetary  savings  of  the  reform  would  range between EUR 355 and 2 107 million. In the absence of labour supply effects, the simulations indicate that the reform would generate between EUR 355 and 839 million savings, depending on the take-up of minimum  income  support.  The  reform,  however,  is  expected  to  incentivise  workers  to  take  up employment  and  when  considering  these  behavioural  responses,  the  budgetary  impact  increases substantially. Under the scenario in which one third of affected individuals transition into employment, net budgetary gains are estimated up to EUR 2.1 billion, driven by higher revenues from personal income taxes  and  social  security  contributions,  and  lower  expenditure  on  minimum  income  support.  These findings  suggest  that  the  labour  market  response  will  be  the  main  determinant  of  the  reform's  fiscal impact. 
The  distributional  impact  of  the  reform  is  expected  to  remain  limited.  Disposable  household income declines only modestly in the absence of labour supply effects, as the minimum income support system offsets a large part of the income loss among affected households. When labour supply effects are included, income losses are substantially reduced and are fully offset for almost all income groups. 
Consequently, the reform is projected to have only a marginal effect on inequality and poverty.  
The  reform  could  help  improve  fiscal  sustainability  while  maintaining  strong  income protection,  particularly  if  accompanied  by  effective  employment  support  measures.  While specific  targeted  measures  are  currently  in  place,  the  effectiveness  and  efficiency  of  these  measures vary  substantially  (Desiere  et  al.,  2010).  For  instance,  a  hiring  subsidy  for  long-term  unemployed  was found  to  have  a  significant  positive  impact  on  the  transition  out  of  unemployment,  but  this  effect  is mainly driven by higher educated participants which are probably the least vulnerable group among the long-term  unemployed  (Desiere  and  Cockx,  2022).  Given  the  substantial  loss  of  skills  during  a  long unemployment spell, up- and re-skilling measures, including on-the-job training are expected to be most effective  (e.g.  Cockx  et  al.,  1998;  Jacobson  et  al.,  2011).  Large  differences  in  socio-economic characteristics  among  the  very  long-term  unemployed  underlines  also  the  importance  of  personalised guidance  and  job  search  support.  In  addition,  early  intervention for  jobseekers  is warranted  to  prevent skills depreciation and social isolation.  
While  the  unemployment  benefit  reform  presents  a  necessary  step  toward  improving  work incentives and reducing long-term unemployment, it remains insufficient to achieve the 80% employment  target.  In  2024,  the  employment  rate  in  Belgium  reached  72.3%,  well  below  the  80% target  for  2030  and  even  in  case  zero  unemployment  is  assumed,  it  would  remain  below  the  target (76.4%).  This  is  linked  to  the  fact  that  Belgium  faces  a  structural  issue  that  extends  beyond  the registered  unemployed:  a  large  share  of  the  working-age  population  remains  outside  the  labour  force altogether.  Therefore,  activating  only  those  receiving  unemployment  benefits  will  not  be  enough  to substantially raise overall employment levels. A comprehensive activation strategy should encompass all inactive  groups,  combining  benefit  reform  with  measures  that  enhance  employability,  and  remove disincentives to participation across all areas of the welfare system.

## FINDING INFORMATION ABOUT THE EU

Online 
Information about the European Union in all the official languages of the EU is available on the Europa website (european-union.europa.eu). 

EU Publications 
You  can  view  or  order  EU  publications  at  op.europa.eu/en/publications.  Multiple  copies  of  free publications  can  be  obtained  by  contacting  Europe  Direct  or  your  local  documentation  centre (european-union.europa .eu/contact-eu/meet-us_en). 

EU law and related documents 
For access to legal information from the EU, including all EU law since 1951 in all the official language versions, go to EUR-Lex (eur-lex.europa.eu). 

Open data from the EU 
The  portal  data.europa.eu  provides  access  to  open  datasets  from  the  EU  institutions,  bodies  and agencies.  These  can  be  downloaded  and  reused  for  free,  for  both  commercial  and  non-commercial purposes. The portal also provides access to a wealth of datasets from European countries.
