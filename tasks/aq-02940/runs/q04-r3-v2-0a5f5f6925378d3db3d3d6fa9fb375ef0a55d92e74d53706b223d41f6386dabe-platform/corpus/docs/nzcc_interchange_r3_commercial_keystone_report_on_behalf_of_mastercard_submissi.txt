1.0 Executive summary
1. This submission has been prepared by Keystone on behalf of Mastercard to support engagement with the New Zealand Commerce Commission (NZCC) in the context of its review of the market for commercial cards in New Zealand. It provides an economic assessment grounded in publicly available evidence, Mastercard internal information, and established principles of platform economics.

2. Keystone (www.Keystone.com) is an economic and strategy consulting firm with offices in the US and London, which focuses on competition matters in tech markets. This report has been authored by Dr Andrea Coscelli, Senior Partner and former Chief Executive Officer of the UK Competition and Markets Authority, Dr George Charlson, Senior Engagement Manager, Francesca Montrasio, Engagement Manager and Paripoorna Baxi, Consultant.

It reallocates value across both sides of the platform and influences participation, usage, pricing, credit provision, service quality, and investment decisions on both the issuing and acquiring sides. Commercial cards also differ materially from consumer cards.9 They are more heavily creditbased, involve larger credit lines and higher spend per-account, and are typically embedded within broader banking and commercial relationships. They are also associated with reconciliation and account-management features,
10 enhanced controls like spend limits and restrictions on types of purchases,

9. Publicly available evidence suggests that commercial cards operate in New Zealand within a competitive and evolving B2B payments ecosystem alongside bank transfers, three-party networks, and emerging fintech and open-banking solutions. In this context, interchange fees play a role in sustaining participation and coordinating incentives across the platform, and changes to it may have effects beyond any immediate change in merchant charges, including impacts on credit provision, usage patterns, and substitution across payment instruments. Importantly, flexible, unregulated interchange ensures competitive neutrality is maintained between four-party networks and three-party networks.

10. The final section of this submission develops an economic framework for assessing how interchange fees sustain participation and investment in commercial card markets. It also explains why, in a two-sided system, capping interchange fees is likely to have system-wide effects (including altered issuer incentives, changes in cardholder usage, and potential shifts by merchants toward alternative payment instruments) rather than simply delivering straightforward reductions in merchant costs. It then reviews international best practice and shows that the most relevant overseas comparators support maintaining greater flexibility for commercial card interchange fees than for consumer cards.

4.0 Competitive dynamics and alternatives in the
B2B payments ecosystem
64. This section provides an overview of the broader B2B payments ecosystem within which commercial cards operate, including the range of alternative payment instruments available to businesses and the evolving competitive landscape. It first describes the different payment instruments used in B2B transactions, and how commercial cards interact with and differ from other instruments such as bank transfers, cheques, and emerging fintech-enabled solutions (Section 4.1). It then considers the dynamic nature of competition in B2B payments, including the role of ongoing innovation, open banking developments, and the entry of new providers in expanding the set of options available to businesses (Section 4.2). Finally, it considers the importance of maintaining competitive neutrality between four-party networks and three-party networks, and the potential for interchange regulation to affect competitive dynamics and substitution between payment systems (Section 4.3).

5.1 Commercial card networks are two-sided platforms in which pricing affects participation on both sides
90. As recognised in the economic literature on payment systems and platform markets, interdependence between the two sides of the market gives rise to indirect network effects, where the attractiveness of participation on each side therefore depends, in part, on the value and participation decisions of the other side.155
91. Because of these cross-side linkages, the overall performance of the platform depends not only on the total level of fees charged within the system, but also on the structure of those fees across participants.156 Economic theory distinguishes between the price level (the aggregate prices charged by the platform for participation and use) and the price structure (how the economic value generated by the platform is allocated between the two sides).157 In two-sided platforms, adjustments to pricing on one side may affect usage, investment, and participation on the other side in ways that are not observed in one-sided markets.

92. In four-party networks, interchange fees serve as a mechanism through which value is balanced and allocated between the issuing side and the acquiring side of the platform. By transferring value from acquirers (and indirectly merchants) to issuers (and cardholders), interchange fees influence the relative prices faced by merchants and commercial cardholders, and thereby the distribution of value between the two sides of the platform. Changes in interchange fees therefore affect issuer revenues per transaction and the net value proposition for merchants, which in turn may influence credit provision, service levels, cardholder pricing, and acceptance decisions.
93. The way in which this value is shared between the two sides may affect incentives to participate.
A greater share of value accruing to issuers may support growth in cardholder adoption, credit provision, fraud management, customer servicing, and investment in functionality. At the same time, the value proposition for merchants remains central to acceptance decisions. Changes in interchange fees that alter this balance may therefore influence both the extent of issuer-side investment and the attractiveness of acceptance to merchants. In this context, flexibility in interchange fees allows the allocation of value between the two sides of the platform to adjust in response to changing market conditions, supporting efficient participation and investment decisions on both sides rather than distorting the underlying economics of the system.
94. These dynamics are particularly relevant in the commercial card segment. As discussed in Section 3.2, commercial cards are characterised by greater reliance on credit, higher transaction values, more concentrated exposure per account, and enhanced service requirements relative to consumer cards. Moreover, the commercial card market is less mature and has lower penetration

5.2.4. Merchants (supplier businesses): incentives to accept and value derived from transactions
118. Merchants face MSFs that include interchange fees as a component. Reductions in interchange fees may lower acceptance costs to the extent they are passed through by acquirers. However, merchant acceptance decisions depend not only on cost but also on value generated. For instance, the previously on-the-margin merchant may realise limited or no benefit from card acceptance due to cardholders no longer receiving sufficient incentives to pay with their card.
119. The interchange fee partly reflects the value of the issuer’s immediate and guaranteed payment to the merchant. Accordingly, the MSF is often referred to as the Merchant Discount Rate (MDR), since the merchant receives payment at a discount in return for this advanced and guaranteed receipt of funds. At the same time, the issuer effectively extends credit to the cardholder and assumes repayment risk between the time of the transaction and settlement.

120. Therefore, the interchange fee should be evaluated not solely as a transactional cost, but in the context of its contribution to supporting the features that merchants value. These features – including faster, more certain payment and reduced reliance on managing receivables – are not limited to large merchants. Card acceptance can enable smaller merchants, who may have more limited access to alternative financing or sophisticated payment infrastructure, to access similar liquidity and risk-management benefits. In this sense, card systems can broaden access to payment and financing functionalities across merchants of different sizes.

121. As laid out in Sections 2 and 3, commercial card transactions are often larger in value, may settle more quickly than certain alternatives (e.g., bank transfers), and reduce counterparty and fraud risk relative to some payment instruments. In B2B contexts, card acceptance may also facilitate procurement processes and attract business customers who rely on card-based purchasing systems. The net merchant incentive depends on the balance between acceptance cost and these value drivers.

122. If changes to interchange fees were to reduce the attractiveness of commercial card programmes and prompt cardholders to substitute towards alternative payment instruments, the net effect for merchants may not necessarily be cost-reducing. As previewed, while attention often focuses on MSFs, a broader economic assessment must consider both direct and indirect costs associated with alternative payment instruments.

123. Firstly, alternative payment instruments are not necessarily cheaper in direct terms. Merchants frequently incur processing fees, scheme fees, or acceptance costs when accepting other cardbased instruments, including three-party networks. In some cases, MSFs for alternative card networks may exceed those associated with four-party commercial card transactions.
Accordingly, substitution from one card network to another may increase, rather than reduce, direct acceptance costs.

124. Further, non-card payment instruments can impose material indirect costs on merchants. Cash and cheques, for example, involve handling, storage, security, reconciliation, and deposit costs.
They also carry higher risks of theft, fraud, and error. Even bank transfers, while often perceived as low-cost, can involve administrative burdens associated with invoice matching, reconciliation, and payment tracking, particularly in high-volume or fragmented B2B environments. Industry studies, including analysis by the European Central Bank, indicate that once labour and

May 21, 2026 40 reconciliation time are taken into account, the unit costs of cash are not consistently lower than non-cash payment instruments and can in some contexts be higher.173
125. Taken together, these considerations suggest that substitution away from commercial cards may impose higher direct or indirect costs on merchants, particularly where commercial cards currently support operational efficiencies or reduce payment risk.

126. Finally, substitution effects may extend beyond shifts to alternative payment instruments. Some businesses may instead revert to using consumer credit cards for business expenditure, as suggested by NBER research.174 More broadly, this highlights that capping interchange fees on commercial cards may reduce the attractiveness of dedicated commercial card programmes, potentially leading to substitution toward less tailored or less efficient payment solutions. Such outcomes would run counter to policy objectives aimed at supporting the adoption of payment instruments that are specifically designed for business use.

5.2.5. Platform-level coordination: critical mass and value rebalancing
127. The interchange fee serves a coordination function in this multi-party system. It supports critical mass by balancing incentives between issuance and acceptance. Because business and merchant value drivers differ (and differ from those in consumer markets), pricing flexibility allows revenue to be allocated in a way that sustains participation across heterogeneous segments.
128. Pricing flexibility also allows four-party networks to support both default interchange rates and commercially negotiated bilateral arrangements between issuers and acquirers. Bilateral arrangements can reflect the specific value exchange, commercial relationship, and risk allocation agreed between participants. Regulating interchange may reduce this flexibility by limiting the scope for parties to negotiate bespoke arrangements and tailor pricing to their particular commercial circumstances.

129. Overall, evidence from jurisdictions that have reduced interchange fees (including, for example, Poland’s reforms)

5.3 International best practice broadly supports maintaining flexibility for commercial cards interchange
