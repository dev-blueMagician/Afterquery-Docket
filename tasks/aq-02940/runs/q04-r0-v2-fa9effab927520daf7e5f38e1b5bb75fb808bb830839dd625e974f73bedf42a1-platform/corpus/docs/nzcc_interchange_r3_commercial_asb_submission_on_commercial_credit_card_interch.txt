Commercial credit card interchange fee regulation for Mastercard and Visa networks - Draft Decision and Reasons Paper
ASB appreciates the opportunity to respond to the Commerce Commission (Commission) draft decision on commercial credit card interchange fee regulation for Mastercard and Visa networks (Draft Decision or 'these proposals'). We support the Commission's aim of promoting a retail payment system for the long-term benefit of merchants and consumers in New Zealand.

We have shared in an appendix our responses to specific questions the Commission has posed. We set out our three key points of feedback and associated recommendations below.
A single flat interchange cap for commercial credit across all issuer locations and card usage environments would be simple and transparent for merchants and acquirers Given the specific features of the commercial credit market compared to personal credit, ASB does not believe there should be a distinct or higher interchange cap for foreign issued cards or card-notpresent cards. Rather, a position should be found which simplifies implementation and evens the playing field.

ASB Recommendation 1: An interchange cap of 1.1% for all market participants is simple for merchants and acquirers to understand, sustains some degree of domestic issuer value and still delivers a top line saving for New Zealand merchants.

A 20bps higher interchange cap on commercial credit (compared to personal credit) suggests the Commission has concluded competition for these services in New Zealand requires less support than in Australia
ASB notes the 50-bps interchange premium for commercial credit cards (versus consumer) that was codified by the Reserve Bank of Australia's latest regulationsl to promote competition and investment.

Appendix: ASB feedback on the targeted questions in the Commercial credit card interchange fee regulation for Mastercard and Visa networks - Draft Decision and Reasons Paper Commerce Commission ‘targeted question’ 2.1: The relevance of Australia in informing specific elements of our approach, such as issuer incentives and sustaining the ability of smaller card issuers (including smaller banks) or open banking providers to compete, and implied weighted-average outcomes.

The Commerce Commission has been heavily informed by Australia’s approach to interchange regulation, without fully acknowledging the key structural differences between the two markets, nor the impacts created by the imposition of the initial 80 bps cap in July 2017. Since that time, commercial credit cards on issue in Australia has dropped 41.8%, the prevalence of charge cards increased and American Express now commands a ~50% commercial card market share, up from 18.2% in July 20172 .

The Reserve Bank of Australia’s commercial interchange cap has created an uneven playing field between unregulated and regulated schemes which has led to reduced competition.

An 80-bps interchange cap in Australia is far less impactful than the same cap in New Zealand due to issuer revenue composition. Australian commercial credit cardholders carry 184% higher interestbearing balances, spend 271% more and on average pay higher fees. As such, the reduced interchange income (via caps) can be more readily absorbed by the wider portfolio economics in Australia than the same cap when applied in New Zealand.

Capping commercial interchange at the same absolute rate in New Zealand will have a disproportionate and more negative impact for issuers in New Zealand that it will/has in Australia.

The initial regulation in 2003 ushered in a period of companion cards, which essentially allowed issuers to offer both three- and four party credit cards to maximise commercial outcomes and cardholder utility. This product construct was a direct result of regulatory asymmetry and should serve as a cautionary example for New Zealand's approach to capping commercial credit cards.
Since 2017, the clear trend in the Australian context has been reduced or capped credit card loyalty earn rates, higher fees (in various forms), higher interest rates and a reduction in product range.
Charge cards have also become more prevalent, offering cardholders reduced functionality compared to a conventional credit card. Commercial credit card propositions have generally seen less change than personal products over the period, primarily due to their higher interchange rates. However, even greater divergence between consumer and commercial markets can be expected once the October 2026 caps on consumer credit cards go live.

ASB notes the 50-bps interchange premium for commercial credit cards (versus consumer) that was codified by the RBA’s latest regulations to promote competition and investment. The Commerce Commission’s 20-bps premium is a clear departure from this approach and suggests that New Zealand’s smaller market, with fewer issuers, needs less competition.

Ultimately, the RBA’s intervention into the Australian market has led to a reduction in the total number of credit cards (consumer and commercial) on issue in Australia, which has fallen by 27.1% since 2017, whilst the population has grown by 16%5
. Such a pronounced reduction in cards on issue represents a material loss of economic value in the form of liquidity and short-term financing for the wider economy.

Commerce Commission ‘targeted question’ 2.3: Key contextual similarities or differences between New Zealand and Australia (including the role of three-party networks) that should affect how these comparisons are applied
The core contextual difference is scale. Australia’s GDP is 7x that of New Zealand’s and materially higher in a per capita basis. The population base is 5x higher, resulting in a Total Addressable Market for an Australian cards issuer that is orders of magnitude higher than in New Zealand. With this scale, Australia can support a wider ecosystem of issuers, from main bank incumbents to new market entrants, such as Airwallex.

The revenue per card for the average commercial credit card in Australia is materially higher than in New Zealand, even with an 80-bps cap on interchange. The combination of a larger market and superior unit level card economics means new and existing card issuers have more scope to compete i.e. they can afford to invest in growth and product augmentation.

The role of American Express is clearly more significant in Australian context. However, we believe the underlying fundamentals are similarly found in New Zealand which enabled American Express to grow so substantially in Australia. We note, American Express has doubled their market share in New Zealand since the implementation of the initial pricing standard in November 20226 . Whilst American
Express remains a smaller player in New Zealand, the Australian example clearly shows rapid and sustained growth is achievable when a regulatory advantage is extended to a market participant.

Commerce Commission 'targeted question' 3.1: We are interested in any views stakeholders may have on our approach at step 3 of our methodology, for example on the additional 10 basis-point premium we have applied above domestic personal credit card interchange to reflect the incremental issuer costs and merchant benefits associated with domestic commercial credit cards ASB welcomes an interchange premium above the consumer credit card caps and agrees with the intention underpinning their inclusion. However, we challenge the size of the premium and do not believe it is enough to achieve its stated goal.

The proposed interchange premium does not address ASB's overarching concerns that the proposed interchange caps will reduce competition and limit cardholder access to credit. There is a risk that issuers reassess their market participation and card propositions if the interchange premium is not appropriate.

Higher interchange levels on commercial credit cards (compared to personal) help to foster a sustainable and competitive market. Evidence of this can be found in the growth of credit card balances since the initial pricing standard was introduced in New Zealand in 2022. Since this time, commercial credit card balances have grown 4%. Over the same period consumer credit cards grew only 0.7%.

ASB would consider a single flat interchange cap across all issuer locations (domestic and foreign) and card usage environments (Card Present and Card Not Present) as an alternative and improved approach, given the specific features of the commercial credit card market. ASB Issuing notes variance between the blended average interchange rate on commercial credit cards when specifically isolating Card Present and Card Not Present transactions. Moreover, an ever-increasing share of commercial credit card transactions are Card Not Present today. For ASB, of commercial credit card transactions occur online (Card Not Present), whilst ASB's consumer credit card book sees A level of approximately 1.1% for all market participants is simple for merchants and acquirers to understand, sustains some degree of domestic issuer value and still delivers a top line saving for New Zealand businesses. ASB notes commercial credit card transactions occur at merchants receiving preferential 'Strategic Merchant' and 'Industry Program' interchange rates, meaning the ASB does not believe there should be a distinct or higher interchange cap for foreign issued cards. Rather a net neutral position should be found which simplifies implementation and evens the playing field.

Commerce Commission 'targeted question' 3.2: The relevance and scale of the incremental issuer costs (fraud losses, fraud prevention measures, and administrative costs).

Cost should be considered in the context of revenue, as larger/heighted costs can be more/less offset depending on the per card income being generated. Simply focusing on the cost differential between consumer and commercial card portfolios reflects only a component of the value exchange. As mentioned in 2.1, the revolve rate on a commercial credit card meaning fewer customers pay interest. As fewer customers pay interest on a commercial credit card than a consumer product, offsetting any lost interchange income via fees and/or interest income would require a disproportionately high degree of cross subsidisation. The costs associated with providing a commercial credit card product are far from limited to fraud. In the 12-months to May 2026, ASB recorded fraud rate rather than the 1 — 2 bps quoted in the consultation 6

7 paper. In addition to fraud, ASB carries a higher degree of credit risk (which is not born by the merchant accepting the payment) on commercial cards vs consumer.

Furthermore, commercial credit cards provide many businesses critical working capital – essentially extending interest-free liquidity to business and providing float. Interchange is the primary mechanism to sustain the issuer-to-cardholder value exchange. The product provides liquidity to business and facilitates a safer and most cost-effective means to transact. However, providing cardholders access to these funds, costs ASB considerably. The Cost of Funds associated with enabling a credit card portfolio is often erroneously considered applicable to revolvers only – those customers carrying a balance and paying interest. This is not the case. Issuers make merchants whole upfront and are repaid by the cardholders in the future. The cost associated with providing these funds Clearly, there are a myriad of direct and allocated costs associated with maintaining a credit card portfolio that would provide little value to state line by line. Rather, ASB would ask the Commission to consider a more rounded (total) cost to income approach to evaluate sustainability rather than anchoring the analysis purely to cost differentials between consumer and commercial cards.
Commerce Commission ‘targeted question’ 4.1: Whether the proposed interchange caps may affect your ability and/or incentives to enter, expand, or compete in commercial credit card issuance; and Commerce Commission ‘targeted question’ 4.2 Potential impacts on pricing, product viability, or investment decisions
The proposed commercial credit card caps may prompt issuers to consider revenue levers within their portfolios and/or cost savings, and may impact issuers’ future commercial credit card investment.
Unlike consumer credit cards, commercial cards are not a requisite product for a credit card issuer.
