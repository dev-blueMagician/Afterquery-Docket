Background
Citibank NA - New Zealand (“Citi”) welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback on the Commerce Commission’s Draft Decision and Reasons Paper in relation to Commercial Credit Card Interchange Fee Regulation (“Draft Decision”).

Citi is a global financial institution with an established presence in New Zealand, providing financial services, including the issuance of Commercial Card products to wholesale clients including public sector clients. We support Commercial Card programmes across multiple jurisdictions and therefore bring experience from a range of market structures and operating environments.

Citi believes that it is important that any regulatory approach reflects both the distinct economics of Commercial Cards and the structural characteristics of the New Zealand market.

Commercial Cards are fundamentally different from consumer credit cards, both in their design and underlying economics. They are typically structured as charge card products, used for higher-value and predictable business expenditures, where balances are settled in full each billing cycle. As such:

-  Commercial Cards do not generate any material interest income

-  Interchange fees represent the primary revenue stream supporting these products

-  Commercial Cards provide integrated payment, data, reporting capabilities, and business process automation efficiencies.

Commercial Cards deliver significant value to corporates, public sector entities, and merchants by:

-  Streamlining accounts payable and expense management processes

-  Providing enhanced transaction data and reporting flows, often integrated directly into Enterprise Resource Planning (“ERP”) systems

-  Supporting working capital optimisation

-  Reducing administrative burden and operational cost, enabling them to re-deploy resources to tasks with higher added value.

These capabilities require ongoing investment in technology, servicing, and integration.
They are a key differentiator compared to consumer credit card offerings.
Executive Summary
We support the Commission’s objective of improving outcomes in the payments ecosystem. However, we are concerned that the Draft Decision, in its current form, may lead to unintended consequences for competition, innovation, and business efficiency.
New Zealand is a smaller and more concentrated market relative to comparator jurisdictions such as Australia. According to World Bank data, New Zealand’s population is approximately 5.3 million compared to approximately 27.2 million in Australia, while New Zealand’s GDP is approximately USD 260 billion compared to approximately USD 1.76 trillion in Australia.

Page 3
This difference in scale is reflected in a narrower issuer base and lower transaction volumes, reducing the market’s ability to absorb regulatory changes without impacting participation and investment.

Against this backdrop, we consider that the Draft Decision:

-  Does not fully reflect the economics of Commercial Cards, which are not revolving products and rely on interchange fees as their primary revenue source.

-  Proposal of a 20 basis point premium to consumer interchange is insufficient, and does not adequately reflect the cost and value structure of Commercial Card programmes.

-  Does not include complementary measures (e.g. surcharging or acquiring transparency), creating uncertainty as to whether the benefits will be passed through.

-  May alter competitive dynamics, including creating relative advantages for threeparty network providers and increasing the risk of structural market shifts over time.

-  May diminish the commercial viability of investment in product innovation, fraud controls, tokenisation and digital wallet capabilities, given the smaller New Zealand market where programme scale is limited.

-  May disproportionately affect SMEs, through lower availability of credit, as issuers tighten credit criteria. This may lead to increased costs to merchants, as buyers request extended payment terms, to offset reduction in working capital benefits provided by Commercial Cards. Additional costs include bad debt collection, manual processing costs, and cash collection costs. Korefusion Research
1 states that Merchants can significantly reduce bad debts, by 106 basis points on average, thanks to a guarantee provided by card acceptance.

-  In a market with a more concentrated issuer base, even modest changes in participation or investment may have a disproportionate impact on competition, choice, and innovation.

Taken together, we consider that the Draft Decision may risk creating a less competitive and less innovative payments ecosystem and may place New Zealand corporates at a relative disadvantage compared to global peers.

Response to Targeted Questions
Question 2. Issuer’s view on Commerce Commission approach at steps 1 and 2 of their methodology:

How does the New Zealand market differ from comparator markets?

Citi notes the Commerce Commission has used the Australian interchange fee as a benchmark for New Zealand interchange. However, Australia's Commercial Card market benefits from materially higher transaction volumes, larger corporate programmes, and a

greater ability to spread fixed technology, compliance and servicing costs. These structural differences reduce the direct comparability of the Australian interchange fee settings.
The Commission's reliance on Australian outcomes therefore risks overstating the ability of the New Zealand market to absorb similar reductions.

Unlike Australia, New Zealand does not include regulation of surcharging or acquiring practices, meaning there is no certainty that interchange reductions will be passed through.
Citi’s view is that this is a material difference between Australia and New Zealand. The result may simply be a redistribution of revenue within the payments value chain rather than a reduction in merchant acceptance costs. Any regulation of Commercial Card interchange fees, should be accompanied by regulatory intervention with regards to acquiring and surcharging fees.

Issuer’s feedback on the additional 10 basis point premium above domestic personal credit card interchange to promote stable and sustainable market outcomes and mitigate the risk of market disruption.

Citi does not view the 10 basis point premium as sufficient to reflect the differences in the products or to prevent market disruption. Given the size of the New Zealand market, the premium may be insufficient to attract issuers to the market and support ongoing investment in innovative products in New Zealand.

Question 3. Issuer’s view on Commerce Commission’s approach at step 3 of their methodology:

We do not consider the proposed 10 basis points premium to domestic personal credit card interchange to be sufficient for Commercial Cards for the following reasons:

-  The proposal does not reflect the absence of interest income on Commercial Cards provided to medium to large corporate and public sector clients; interchange is the primary source of revenue.

-  The proposal does not adequately account for higher servicing and capability requirements for Commercial Card products which require set up of client-specific configurations, controls, approval chains and data file feeds.

-  Commercial Card programmes provide a unique two-sided working capital benefit to both buyers and suppliers through the use of the issuer's balance sheet. Buyers receive extended payment terms while merchants typically receive payment within one to two days of transaction settlement. The extended payment terms Commercial Cards provide buyers, provides suppliers with the opportunity to modify payment terms when they accept cards. KoreFusion research paper estimates the potential benefit at approximately 15 days of accelerated payment2.
This creates a valuable source of liquidity for both parties and reduces the need for alternative financing arrangements. Regulatory intervention that reduces the economics supporting these programmes may weaken benefits for participants.

-  Citi is of the view that the cost of funding interest free periods should be considered in the interchange rate, given the sharing of the working capital benefits to the merchant. A material change in interchange will reduce the ability of issuers to continue providing these working capital benefits under current terms, potentially leading to shorter payment cycles, reduced credit availability and increased financing costs for New Zealand businesses.

Difference in approach for In-person and Online card use Citi notes that implementing two distinct interchange rates for in-person and online is a meaningful variation to the Reserve Bank of Australia’s approach. This will provide significant pricing complexity to issuers as each client will have different in-person and online spend patterns. This differentiation, coupled with the limiting mechanism, could be significantly lower that then assumed 78bps implied weighted average in New Zealand.
Question 4. Issuer’s views on the impacts on competition The Draft Decision may have adverse impacts on competition for the following reasons:
Three-party payment network advantage

-  Three-party payment networks are not subject to the same constraints, yet they target the same flows and proposition. As such they will gain a relative competitive advantage, particularly in the commercial segment, accelerating migration towards the three-party payment networks.

-  While American Express may currently represent a relatively small share of the overall New Zealand payments market, its position within commercial payments is growing. The Draft Decision will create an uneven playing field.

-  The three-party payment network advantage may ultimately create the need for future regulatory intervention to address distortions in competitive outcomes that could be avoided through more proportionate settings today.

Issuer participation

-  Existing issuers may scale back their offerings or potentially exit the market given the significantly lower interchange fees they would be able to charge in New Zealand.

-  The Draft Decision may result in the only issuers remaining in New Zealand being those who are able to cross subsidise across other aspects of their business.
Innovation and digital competition
Investment by issuers of Commercial Cards into innovative products such as digital wallets, tokenisation and virtual cards integration into wallets, could be constrained given the lower revenue streams issuers will face.

Commercial Card propositions require ongoing investment in fraud prevention, cyber resilience, authentication capabilities and transaction monitoring to address increasingly sophisticated financial crime threats.

These capabilities are increasingly central to modern corporate payment solutions, and reduced investment may limit innovation, adoption and security for New Zealand corporates compared to their international peers.

Question 9. Issuer’s view on whether the proposed two-to-three month implementation period is sufficient to implement required pricing and system changes.

A two-to-three month implementation period would not be achievable for many issuers and corporate clients.

Any material restructuring of programme economics may require changes to client contractual agreements, systems, reporting processes and accounting workflows which could take multiple system releases to fully encapsulate, in the world of corporate technology development.

For this reason, Citi recommends a minimum implementation period of twelve months, mapped to four major quarterly technology releases if the Draft Decision is implemented.
Summary of Citi’s Recommendations:

-  Continue to exclude Commercial Card interchange from regulatory cap, or if the Commerce Commission decides to proceed with implementing a cap then implement a materially higher Commercial Card interchange differential than that proposed in the Draft Decision

-  Introduce complementary measures addressing acquiring transparency and surcharging before implementing interchange reductions

-  Reconsider the proposed differentiation between in person and online Commercial Card interchange rates

-  Allow a minimum of 12 months implementation period Conclusion
While the objectives of the proposal are acknowledged, we consider that, in its current form, it may:

-  Misalign with Commercial Card economics

-  Reduce competition and innovation

-  Fail to ensure achieved benefits are passed through

-  Lead to product simplification or reduced availability

-  Lead to a reduction in fraud protection and its future enhancements

-  Create a long-term disadvantage for competitiveness of New Zealand businesses

Changes to interchange settings may alter competitive dynamics, including by increasing the relative attractiveness of three-party payments network. This creates a risk of structural shifts in the market that should instead be addressed at the outset.
We therefore encourage the Commission to adopt an approach that reflects the distinct characteristics of Commercial Cards and supports a competitive, innovative, and efficient commercial payments ecosystem.
