Submission to the Commerce Commission on its Draft Decisions and Reasons Paper relating to Commercial Credit Card Interchange Fee Regulation for Mastercard and Visa Networks
1. Kiwibank welcomes the opportunity to provide feedback to the Commerce Commission (Commission) on its Draft Decisions and Reasons Paper relating to Commercial Credit Card Interchange Fee Regulation for Mastercard and Visa Networks (Draft Decision).
2. This submission focusses on the proposed interchange fee caps for domestic commercial credit cards (CCC Interchange Caps). Kiwibank’s key points are:
(a) The Commission should not proceed with further interchange regulation until it can demonstrate clear and evidenced benefits for customers.

(b) The proposed CCC Interchange Caps are too low. The methodology used to determine them does not sufficiently account for impact of strategic merchant pricing, the New Zealand context, the costs of issuing commercial cards or the benefits commercial card products deliver to customers and merchants.

(c) The Draft Decision will disproportionately impact smaller banks.

(d) More time is required to implement the Draft Decision.

Interchange fee caps have not generated benefits for customers
3. The Commission should not proceed with further interchange regulation until it can demonstrate clear and evidenced benefits for customers. While we recognise the objective of reducing merchant costs, there is no evidence that existing interchange caps have translated into improved outcomes for customers through lower retail prices or reduced surcharging.

4. Surcharging remains prevalent following the introduction and further reduction of interchange caps, calling into question the extent of pass-through to end users. Data provided by , a large terminal provider, shows that:

(a) The total percentage of transactions surcharged increased following the implementation of the initial pricing standard in 2022, and continued to increase following the further reduction of interchange fees in late 2025.

(b) The average surcharge amount has not changed significantly since 2022.
(c) The total number of terminals with surcharging enabled has continued to increase since 2022, and reached a record high in May 2026.

5. Lower interchange revenue is likely to result in issuers adjusting pricing and reducing rewards, as observed following the decision to reduce interchange fees in 2025. Further reductions therefore risk imposing costs on business customers without delivering corresponding benefits – at a time when economic conditions are already challenging for SMEs.

The proposed CCC Interchange Caps are too low
6. The proposed CCC Interchange Caps are too low. The methodology used to determine them does not adequately reflect the impact of strategic merchant pricing, the New Zealand context, the costs of issuing commercial cards or the benefits commercial cards deliver to customers and merchants. The Commission’s three step analysis places

2 insufficient weight on several key factors, resulting in CCC Interchange Caps that risk under‐compensating issuers and undermining the sustainability of current commercial credit card offerings.

Step 1: Starting with the benchmarks
7. The Commission should more fully consider the position in the UK and EU before deciding whether to adopt the CCC Interchange Caps. The Commission acknowledges that commercial credit card interchange fees remain unregulated in the EU and UK.
However, the Commission has excluded the UK and EU as benchmark jurisdictions, rather than considering the reasons commercial credit card interchange fees remain unregulated in those jurisdictions. The UK and EU have treated commercial cards differently from consumer cards because of their distinct economics and business use cases. These jurisdictions provide an important counterpoint to the proposed approach and suggest caution in adopting relatively tight caps.

Step 2: Considering the New Zealand context
8. The Draft Decision does not sufficiently account for:

(a) the impact of strategic merchant pricing on the implied weighted average domestic commercial credit card interchange fee; or
(b) the New Zealand context.

Strategic merchant pricing
9. The Commission’s analysis does not account for the impact of strategic merchant pricing, meaning the real‐world effects of the proposed caps are likely to be more pronounced than anticipated. A significant proportion of transactions occur at negotiated or preferential rates below headline interchange levels. For example, Government interchange fees are 0.20%. The Commission is intending to implement a limiting mechanism to prevent these strategic merchant rates from being raised. Accordingly, it is important that these rates are accounted for in its calculations.

10. When strategic merchant rates are accounted for, Kiwibank estimates that its implied weighted average domestic commercial credit card interchange fee would be approximately 0.63% - far lower than the Commission’s estimate of 0.78%. Once strategic merchant rates are considered, the implied weighted average domestic commercial credit card interchange fee in New Zealand is likely to be far lower than in Australia if the proposed CCC Interchange Caps are applied.

The New Zealand context
11. The Commission’s analysis does not identify any compelling reasons for interchange fees to be lower in New Zealand than Australia. Conversely, there are several rational reasons they may be higher. Despite this, the Draft Decision proposes to impose CCC Interchange Caps that, together with the proposed limiting mechanism, are likely to result in lower weighted average fees than in Australia.

12. The proposed caps do not sufficiently reflect the smaller scale of the New Zealand market, which increases the relative cost of issuing and servicing commercial credit products. Compared to larger markets such as Australia, fixed and operational costs are spread over a smaller transaction base, meaning costs are higher in proportion to revenue. This limits issuers’ ability to absorb reductions in interchange and increases

3 the likelihood that costs will be passed through to customers through higher fees or reduced product features.

13. At paragraph 2.37 of the Draft Decision, the Commission states that in jurisdictions with many small businesses that also act as merchants, transparency and surcharging are less effective at constraining commercial credit card pricing than in jurisdictions where commercial credit card usage is more concentrated among larger or medium‐sized merchants with greater bargaining power. We note that the Commission’s only benchmark jurisdiction is Australia, which has a similar proportion of firms classified as SMEs. Accordingly, this factor does not indicate that more restrictive interchange fee caps may be required in New Zealand compared to Australia.

14. Finally, the Commission has not given adequate weight to the likelihood that American Express will grow its market share as a result of the Draft Decision. The Draft Decision will grant American Express a material advantage. It is unclear why the Commission believes that, although American Express has grown its market share in Australia under current interchange caps, its Draft Decision is unlikely to cause a similar shift in New Zealand. It is also unclear why competitors carrying out substantially similar activities should be regulated differently, in a way that advantages one competitor.
Step 3: Considering relevant issuer costs and merchant benefits
15. A 0.20% premium is insufficient to reflect long run average net credit and fraud losses.
We understand that, as part of its analysis, the Commission requested average net fraud and credit losses for a 12-month period from issuers. 16. The Commission should consider average net fraud and credit losses over a longer period to obtain a more accurate view of the long run averages.

17. Additionally, in its analysis the Commission gives insufficient weight to the role of rewards and value‐added services, which benefit merchants as well as cardholders.
Rewards programmes incentivise card use and support higher transaction volumes. This can drive additional sales for merchants. Treating these benefits as solely accruing to cardholders underestimates their broader value within the payments ecosystem.
The Draft Decision will disproportionately impact smaller banks
18. The Commission should reconsider its Draft Decision in light of its findings in the final report for its market study into personal banking services (Market Study Report). In our previous submissions on interchange fee caps, we highlighted that interchange fee caps have a disproportionate impact on smaller banks, which have smaller economies of scale to absorb additional costs. The Draft Decision considers the ability of small issuers to enter and compete in commercial credit card markets, but does not consider potential impacts on banking competition more broadly.

4
19. As noted in our previous submissions, absorbing the financial impacts of interchange fee caps reduces our ability to invest in growth and innovation. Kiwibank and other smaller card issuers are more impacted than larger card issuers, which have greater ability to absorb financial impacts. This disparity is likely to be more pronounced in the context of commercial credit cards, which have larger fixed servicing costs comparative to payment volumes.

20. Additionally, to the extent card issuers are required to absorb costs, the proposed interchange fees are unlikely to “enhance allocative, productive and dynamic efficiency”.
Instead, they may lead to cross subsidisation of payment services.

More time is required to implement the Draft Decision
21. The implementation period for any CCC Interchange Caps should be at least six months from the final decision. A two to three month implementation period is not sufficient. In order to implement CCC Interchange Caps, issuers will need to reassess and, in some cases, recalibrate commercial arrangement to ensure that products remain viable. A six month implementation period would align with the implementation period for the decisions in relation to domestic personal cards, with some additional time to recognise that some implementation activities cannot occur over December and January.
22. The Draft Decisions states that the Commission considers “this advance notice of our proposed implementation timing, alongside our previous intent to regulate commercial credit cards in the July 2025 decision paper, strikes an appropriate balance.” However, this overlooks the fact that issuers cannot take steps reassess their commercial arrangements until a final decision is issued.
