ORDER GRANTING MOTION TO DISMISS
Our Plaintiff, Rahul Bagga, has sued Royal Caribbean Cruises Ltd. (“RCCL”), asserting claims of negligent maintenance, negligent failure to warn, and negligent design. See generally Complaint [ECF No. 1]. RCCL has moved to dismiss this action “because the Ticket Contract between [Bagga] and RCCL contains a forum selection clause that requires all suits for personal injuries arising out of the subject cruise to be litigated, if at all, in and before the Courts of England and Wales.” Motion to Dismiss (the “Motion”) [ECF No. 13] at 1. After careful review, we GRANT the Motion.

THE LAW
Forum non conveniens “is an ancient common law doctrine that permits a court to decline jurisdiction over a case, even if personal jurisdiction and venue are otherwise proper, when there is a more convenient forum for the case to be litigated.” Esfeld v. Costa Crociere, S.P.A., 289 F.3d 1300, 1302 n.4 (11th Cir. 2002); see also Boyd v. Deadwood Tobacco Co., 2024 WL 940822, at *2 (S.D. Fla. Mar. 5, 2024) (Altman, J.) (“Under the doctrine of forum non conveniens [ ], a district court has the inherent power to decline to exercise jurisdiction even when venue is proper.” (cleaned up)). In a typical forum non conveniens case, “[t]he moving party must demonstrate that (1) an adequate alternative forum is available, (2) the public and private interest factors weigh in favor of dismissal, and (3) the plaintiff can reinstate his suit in the alternative forum without undue inconvenience or prejudice.” Leon v. Millon Air, Inc., 251 F.3d 1305, 1310–11 (11th Cir. 2001).

“Where there is a valid forum-selection clause, however, the forum non conveniens analysis is modified in three consequential ways. First, the plaintiff’s choice of forum merits no weight. Rather, as the party defying the forum-selection clause, the plaintiff bears the burden of establishing that dismissal for forum non conveniens is unwarranted. Second, a court should not consider arguments about the parties’ private interests and must deem the private-interest factors to weigh entirely in favor of the preselected forum. Third, a court in the contractually selected venue should not apply the law of Case 1:26-cv-20364-RKA Document 30 Entered on FLSD Docket 09/14/2026 Page 2 of 12

3 the transferor venue to which the parties waived their right, and this in turn may affect the publicinterest considerations.” Usme v. CMI Leisure Mgmt., Inc., 106 F.4th 1079, 1086 (11th Cir. 2024).
As a result of these modifications, “the Supreme Court has said that the existence of a valid and enforceable forum-selection clause is essentially dispositive in the forum non conveniens analysis.”
Ibid. (citing Atl. Marine Const. Co. v. U.S. Dist. Ct. for W. Dist. of Tex., 571 U.S. 49, 62–64 (2013)); see also GDG Acquisitions, LLC v. Government of Belize, 749 F.3d 1024, 1028 (11th Cir. 2014) (noting that “an enforceable forum-selection clause carries near-determinative weight” in the forum non conveniens analysis).

“A court may ‘consider matters outside the pleadings if presented in proper form by the parties’ in ruling on a motion to dismiss for forum non conveniens.” McGregor v. VP Recs., 2017 WL 2833444, at *3 (S.D. Fla. June 30, 2017) (Bloom, J.) (quoting MGC Commc’ns, Inc. v. BellSouth Telecomms., Inc., 146 F. Supp. 2d 1344, 1349 (S.D. Fla. 2001) (Gold, J.)); see also Turner v. Costa Crociere S.p.A., 9 F.4th 1341, 1346 (11th Cir. 2021) (holding that an Italian attorney’s affidavit was properly considered on motion to dismiss for forum non conveniens). A court may also consider exhibits attached to a motion to dismiss, and “if the allegations of the complaint about a particular exhibit conflict with the contents of the exhibit itself, the exhibit controls.” Hoefling v. City of Miami, 811 F.3d 1271, 1277 (11th Cir. 2016).

I. The Forum-Selection Clause
Under federal maritime law, “[f]orum selection clauses ‘are presumptively valid and enforceable unless the plaintiff makes a strong showing that enforcement would be unfair or unreasonable under the circumstances.’” Turner, 9 F.4th at 1345 (quoting Rucker v. Oasis Legal Fin., L.L.C., 632 F.3d 1231, 1235 (11th Cir. 2011)). “A plaintiff can defeat this presumption by showing that (1) the clause was induced by fraud or overreaching; (2) the plaintiff would be deprived of its day in court because of inconvenience or unfairness; (3) the chosen law would deprive the plaintiff of a remedy; or (4) enforcement of the clause would contravene public policy.” Ibid. (cleaned up). Bagga invokes only the second and third exceptions.

2
a. Inconvenience
First, Bagga argues that litigating in the Courts of England and Wales would be inconvenient for him as a Canadian citizen. See Response at 3 (“In order to present even his own testimony in a hypothetical English or Welsh proceeding, [Bagga] would still have to travel in person across the Atlantic, incurring the associated air travel and hotel costs and visa procedures, or accept the suboptimal solution of a fully electronic and fully remote proceeding[.] . . . If he wished to present the testimony of other lay or expert witnesses in person, he would also have to make arrangements for and incur the costs of their travel to the United Kingdom.”). But “a plaintiff seeking to establish that a forum selection clause is unenforceable due to inconvenience or unfairness bears a heavy burden of proof.” Turner, 9 F.4th at 1345–46 (cleaned up). And a plaintiff relying on “inconveniences that were foreseeable at the time of contracting in order to meet this burden can prevail only by showing that trial in the contractual forum will be so gravely difficult and inconvenient that he will for all practical purposes be deprived of his day in court.” Id. at 1346 (cleaned up). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in Turner is instructive. In that case, the Eleventh Circuit enforced an Italian forum-selection clause even though the plaintiff was a U.S. resident and the vessel had departed from a U.S. port. In so holding, the court found persuasive an affidavit from an Italian attorney explaining that the plaintiff could attend routine Italian proceedings remotely—thus obviating the plaintiff’s concerns about the inconvenience of international travel. See Turner, 9 F.4th at 1346 (“Turner still has not met his burden of proving that pursuit of his claims in Italy would subject him to fundamental unfairness. The reason is basic: he has not established that he would have to travel to Italy in order to pursue his case. The [d]efendants produced an affidavit from an Italian attorney explaining that Turner would not be required to attend routine proceedings in person and that even for those events that required attendance, he could possibly either arrange for appointment of a special attorney to attend on his behalf or request that the event take place in the United States via international rogatory.”).

So too here. RCCL has provided a declaration from an English solicitor explaining that Bagga can pursue his claims in the Courts of England and Wales, in large part, remotely. But these circumstances are not the type of uncontemplated inconveniences that justify the invalidation of a forum selection clause.”).

II. Forum Non Conveniens
But that’s not the end of our inquiry. Having established that the parties’ forum-selection clause is enforceable, “we must now apply a modified forum non conveniens analysis and assess (1) whether an adequate alternative forum is available, (2) whether the public factors weigh in favor of dismissal, and (3) whether the plaintiff can reinstate his suit in the alternative forum without undue inconvenience or prejudice.” Boyd, 2024 WL 940822, at *8 (cleaned up)). All three of these modified factors favor RCCL.

First, the Courts of England and Wales are an adequate alternative forum for this dispute. See M/S Bremen v. Zapata Off-Shore Co., 407 U.S. 1, 12 (1972) (“Plainly, the courts of England meet the standards of neutrality and long experience in admiralty litigation.”). RCCL’s English solicitor attests to the independence and fairness of the Courts of England and Wales and tells us that “many parties to maritime disputes choose to litigate in England and Wales as a neutral jurisdiction with a specialist Admiralty Court[.] . . . As a result, a vast number of personal injury disputes arising from onboard ships have been heard in the Courts of England and Wales over the years.” Iyer Decl. ¶ 37. Bagga never argues that the Courts of England and Wales wouldn’t be an adequate alterative forum—indeed, he concedes that “the [C]ourts of England and Wales are no doubt competent to adjudicate civil disputes in general[.]” Response at 2. The first factor thus favors dismissal.

Second, the public-interest factors likewise favor dismissal. The relevant public-interest factors include “(1) the administrative difficulties flowing from court congestion; (2) the local interest in having localized controversies decided at home; (3) the interest in having the trial . . . in a forum that is at home with the law that must govern the action; (4) the avoidance of unnecessary problems in conflict of laws, or in the application of foreign law; and (5) the unfairness of burdening citizens in an unrelated forum with jury duty.” Boyd, 2024 WL 940822, at *9 (cleaned up) (citing Piper Aircraft Co. v.
Reyno, 454 U.S. 235, 241 n.6 (1981)). When weighing these factors, “the burden is on the plaintiff to Case 1:26-cv-20364-RKA Document 30 Entered on FLSD Docket 09/14/2026 Page 9 of 12

10 show that dismissal of the complaint is unwarranted.” Pappas v. Kerzner Int’l Bahamas Ltd., 585 F. App’x 962, 964 (11th Cir. 2014).

Bagga doesn’t even try to meet his burden of showing that the public-interest factors favor litigating this case in Florida. See generally Response (silent as to public-interest factors). RCCL, on the other hand, claims that “[t]his dispute has little, if any, connection to the Southern District of Florida:
[Bagga] is a citizen and resident of Canada, the Icon of the Seas is a Bahamas-flagged vessel owned by a Liberian entity, and the incident is alleged to have occurred while the vessel was in navigable waters of the Western Caribbean, not in Florida.” Reply at 7. RCCL also tells us that “the Ticket Contract’s choice-of-law clause designat[es] English law” as governing the parties’ dispute—a contention Bagga doesn’t dispute. Motion at 7; see also Iyer Decl. ¶ 10 (“The [T]icket [C]ontract was subject to an exclusive English law and jurisdiction of the Courts of England and Wales clause [ ] otherwise known as a forum selection and choice of law clause.”); id. ¶ 11 (attaching choice-of-law clause specifying that English law will govern all disputes arising from or related to the Ticket Contract).
After careful consideration, we agree with RCCL that the balance of public-interest factors weigh in favor of dismissal. First, even though neither party provided information regarding the comparative administrative congestion between the Courts of England and Wales and the Southern District of Florida, we know that the Southern District of Florida is one of the busiest and most congested court systems in the country. See Gordon v. Sandal Resorts Int’l, Ltd., 418 F. Supp. 3d 1132, 1142 (S.D. Fla. 2019) (Scola, J.) (“[T]he Southern District of Florida has one of the busiest dockets in the country[.]”). It’s therefore unlikely that Bagga would face more congestion in England than in Florida. See Aviation One of Fla., Inc. v. Airborne Ins. Consultants, 722 F. App’x 870, 885 (11th Cir. 2018) (affirming dismissal where district court found that “it was unlikely that [the appellant] would face more congestion in England than in Florida”). Second, the “local interest in having localized controversies decided at home,” id. at 885, is at best neutral, since neither party resides in Florida and Case 1:26-cv-20364-RKA Document 30 Entered on FLSD Docket 09/14/2026 Page 10 of 12

11
Bagga’s injuries didn’t occur here. Third, the Courts of England and Wales are undoubtedly more familiar than we are with English law, which will govern this dispute. See ibid. (“[T]he court determined that the last factor favored litigation in England because the retainer agreement included a choice-oflaw provision designating English law, with which English courts would obviously be more familiar.
Based on this analysis, the court concluded that the public-interest factors failed to overcome the weight of the forum-selection clause, making dismissal appropriate.”). Fourth, the Courts of England and Wales have a strong public interest in hearing cases the parties have agreed to litigate in those courts. See Jiangsu Hongyuan Pharm. Co. v. DI Glob. Logistics Inc., 159 F. Supp. 3d 1316, 1328 (S.D. Fla.
2016) (Gayles, J.) (“China has a stronger interest in having this dispute litigated in its courts, as the parties contracted to litigate in China[.]”). And fifth, RCCL’s English solicitor tells us that “trials of civil claims in England and Wales, including personal injury claims, take place before a judge sitting without a jury,” thus obviating any concern we might have about burdening an English jury with this dispute. Iyer Decl. ¶ 18. For the foregoing reasons, and because Bagga hasn’t advanced any argument on the public-interest factors, we find that the public-interest factors favor dismissal.
Finally, we find that Bagga “can reinstate his suit in the alternative forum without undue inconvenience or prejudice,” Leon, 251 F.3d at 1311, because “RCCL has agreed to waive any statuteof-limitations defense arising from the Ticket Contract’s one-year suit-filing deadline, provided Plaintiff refiles his action in the Courts of England and Wales within six months of any order of dismissal,” Reply at 7–8.

The upshot is that “the existence of a valid and enforceable forum-selection clause is essentially dispositive in the forum non conveniens analysis.” Usme, 106 F.4th at 1086. To overcome the forum-selection clause, Bagga bore the burden “to show that dismissal of the complaint is unwarranted” based on certain public interest factors. See Pappas, 585 F. App’x at 964. He didn’t carry Case 1:26-cv-20364-RKA Document 30 Entered on FLSD Docket 09/14/2026 Page 11 of 12

12 that burden. He didn’t even try. Bagga’s Response addressed only the enforceability of the forumselection clause and never meaningfully addressed forum non conveniens. See Response at 2 (mentioning “forum non conveniens” only once). This failure warrants dismissal.

CONCLUSION
Because the parties agreed to an enforceable forum-selection clause requiring disputes to be litigated in the Courts of England and Wales, we dismiss Bagga’s Complaint under the doctrine of forum non conveniens. Accordingly, we ORDER and ADJUDGE as follows:

1. The Motion to Dismiss [ECF No. 13] is GRANTED.

2. The Complaint [ECF No. 1] is DISMISSED without prejudice.

3. The Clerk is directed to CLOSE this case. All pending deadlines are TERMINATED and any pending motions are DENIED as moot.
