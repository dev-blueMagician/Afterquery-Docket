II. LEGAL STANDARD
A. Anti-Injunction Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2283.

Since its inception, our nation has operated under a dual system of federal and state courts.
Yet, an inherent tension exists between them just by nature of their parallel functions. Chick Kam Choo v. Exxon Corp., 486 U.S. 140, 146 (1988). Although federal courts possess authority to “issue all writs necessary or appropriate in aid of their respective jurisdictions[,]” 28 U.S.C.
§ 1651, to prevent federal courts from encroaching upon state court matters, the Judiciary Act of 1793 recognized that no “writ of injunction [shall] be granted to stay proceedings in any court of state.” Act of Mar. 2, 1793, ch. 22, § 5, 1 Stat. 335. Later, in 1948, Congress formally codified this principle under 28 U.S.C. § 2283—known as the Anti-Injunction Act—to promote and preserve a balance between judicial systems. Chick Kam Choo, 486 U.S. at 146; 28 U.S.C. § 2283. At its Case 4:23-cv-00415-O Document 110 Filed 09/14/26 Page 4 of 12 PageID <pageID>

- 5 -
core, the Act’s “message is one of respect for state courts.” Smith v. Bayer Corp., 564 U.S. 299,
306 (2011).

The text of the Anti-Injunction Act is quite clear: “A court of the United States may not grant an injunction to stay proceedings in a State court.” 28 U.S.C. § 2283 (emphasis added).
Congress did, however, carve out three exceptions for when a federal court may enjoin a state court from pursuing adjudication over a claim: (1) if it is expressly authorized by Act of Congress,
(2) where necessary in aid of its jurisdiction, and (3) to protect or effectuate a federal court’s judgments. Id. The Supreme Court directs lower courts to narrowly construe these exceptions— “not [to] be enlarged by loose statutory construction.” Chick Kam Choo, 486 U.S. at 146 (quoting Atlantic Coast Line R. Co. v. Locomotive Engineers, 389 U.S. 281, 287 (1970)). Lower courts are instructed that “any doubts as to the propriety of a federal injunction against state court proceedings should be resolved in favor of permitting the state courts to proceed.” Smith, 564 U.S. at 306 (quoting Atlantic Coast Line R. Co., 398 U.S. at 297).

Applicable here is the third exception, also known as the relitigation exception, which rests on the principles of res judicata. Vasquez v. Bridgestone/Firestone, Inc., 325 F.3d 665, 675 (5th Cir. 2003). The basic idea is that a party cannot relitigate a claim in state court once a federal court has already adjudicated the matter. Chick Kam Choo, 486 U.S. at 147 (“The relitigation exception was designed to permit a federal court to prevent state litigation of an issue that previously was presented to and decided by the federal court.”). To preserve the federal court’s judgment, under this exception, a federal court may issue an injunction barring the state court from considering the claim. Like res judicata, the relitigation exception requires that (1) the parties in the state action must be identical (or at least in privity with) the parties in the previous federal action, (2) the judgment in the prior action must have been rendered by a court of competent jurisdiction, (3) the Case 4:23-cv-00415-O Document 110 Filed 09/14/26 Page 5 of 12 PageID <pageID>

- 6 -
prior action must have concluded with a final judgment on the merits, and (4) the same claim or cause of action must be involved in both suits. New York Life Ins. Co. v. Gillispie, 203 F.3d 384,
387 (5th Cir. 2000) (quoting United States v. Shanbaum, 10 F.3d 305, 310 (5th Cir. 1994)).
Importantly, the previous claim before the federal court must have been actually decided for the exception to apply. Smith, 564 U.S. at 302 (“the issue presented in the state court [has to be] identical to the one decided in the federal tribunal”); Texas Com. Bank Nat. Ass’n v. State of Fla., 138 F.3d 179, 182 (5th Cir. 1998) (“an issue must be ‘actually litigated’ in federal court before an injunction can issue under the relitigation exception”). For example, in Chick Kam Choo the Supreme Court found that the relitigation exception did not apply because the federal court’s order rested its analysis on forum non conveniens grounds. Chick Kam Choo, 486 U.S. at 150. The state court was not barred from hearing the case simply because the federal court had deemed the claims nonjusticiable. Id. Because the federal court had not decided the claims on the merits, it became the state court’s responsibility to decide whether the claim before them was reviewable.
Id. at 151.

The same principle can be applied to the doctrine of preclusion. When a court applies the preclusion doctrines of res judicata or collateral estoppel, it does not evaluate, consider, nor adjudicate the underlying claims. Rather the court asks whether the claim itself has been litigated before. A decision on the merits answers the substance of the dispute, whereas a nonjusticiable ruling is a determination preventing the court from reaching those claims in the first place.
Applying Chick Kam Choo to the question of preclusion, a federal court may not enjoin a state court when the federal court’s previous order rested its judgment on preclusion grounds; so, it is up to the “the state court [to] decide the preclusion question.” Smith, 564 U.S. at 318.

B. All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1561.

The All Writs Act, an equitable instrument, allows federal courts to “issue all writs necessary or appropriate” to aid their jurisdiction and to protect their judgments. 28 U.S.C. § 1651.
“[B]ecause federal courts, being courts of limited jurisdiction, would not otherwise possess the tools necessary to implement their jurisdictional grants[,]” the All Writs Act is a powerful tool possessed by the federal courts. ITT Community Dev. Corp. v. Barton, 569 F.2d 1351, 1359 (5th Cir. 1978). The origins of the All Writs Act can be traced back to legal principles that predate the founding, and like the Anti-Injunction Act, the All Writs Act can be found in founding era legislation. See Jud. Act 1789, § 14. This law was later codified by Congress in 28 U.S.C. § 1651 (“The Supreme Court and all courts established by Act of Congress may issue all writs necessary or appropriate in aid of their respective jurisdictions and agreeable to the usage and principles of law.”).

The Fifth Circuit has instructed that the “scope of . . . the All Writs Act depends on the nature of the case before the court and the legitimacy of the ends sought to be achieved through the exercise of the power.” In re Lease Oil Antitrust Litigation No. II, 48 F.Supp.2d 699, 703 (S.D.
Tex. 1998) (quoting ITT Community Dev. Corp. v. Barton, 569 F.2d 1351, 1358–59 (5th Cir.
1978)) (cleaned up). Like the Anti-Injunction Act exceptions, any use of the All Writs Act by a court must be applied narrowly. United States v. New York Telephone Co., 434 U.S. 159, 172–73 (1977). Specifically, any injunction issued via the All Writs Act shall be “directed at conduct which, left unchecked, would have had the practical effect of diminishing the court’s power to bring the litigation to its natural conclusion.” Barton, 569 F.2d at 1358–59. Thus, if a litigant’s Case 4:23-cv-00415-O Document 110 Filed 09/14/26 Page 7 of 12 PageID <pageID>

- 8 -
conduct is “not shown to be detrimental to the court’s jurisdiction or exercise thereof” the litigant cannot be enjoined by a court under the Act. Id. at 1359.

III. ANALYSIS
A. The Anti-Injunction Act’s Re-Litigation Exception Does Not Apply Because this Court Did Not Adjudicate the Claim on the Merits.

JLF Defendants urge this Court to apply the relitigation exception of the Anti-Injunction Act to enjoin Rhode Island Superior Court from adjudicating the Motion to Disburse filed by Deaton in June 2025.

JLF Defendants argue that because Plaintiff’s claims have already been adjudicated during the Texas arbitration and because this Court dismissed Plaintiff’s previous claim on res judiciata grounds, that the relitigation exception applies.6 Plaintiff does not deny that he brought claims against JLF Defendants in this Court in 2022.7 Plaintiff instead argues that the relitigation exception does not apply because this Court did not actually adjudicate the claim on the merits.8 The Court agrees with the Plaintiff.

JLF Defendants have not established that all four elements of the relitigation exception of the Anti-Injunction Act are met. JLF Defendants had to establish that (1) the parties in the state action are identical (or at least in privity with) the parties in the previous federal action, (2) the judgment in the prior action was rendered by a court of competent jurisdiction, (3) the prior action concluded with a final judgment on the merits, and (4) the same claim or cause of action was involved in both suits. New York Life Ins. Co. v. Gillispie, 203 F.3d 384, 387 (5th Cir. 2000) (quoting United States v. Shanbaum, 10 F.3d 305, 310 (5th Cir. 1994)).

2. JLF Defendants’ claim falls short as to the third element.

It is the third element that bars this court from enjoining Rhode Island Superior Court from deciding Plaintiff’s present Motion to Disburse. The Supreme Court has ordered lower courts to “assess[] the precise state of the record and what the earlier federal order actually said; it [does] not permit the District Court to render a post hoc judgment as to what the order was intended to say.” Chick Kam Choo, 486 U.S. at 148 (citing Atl. Coast Line R. Co., 398 U.S. 281) (emphasis in original). Turning to this Court’s previous Order, this Court dismissed with prejudice all of Plaintiff’s claims on res judiciata grounds, concluding that the Texas Final Arbitration Award precluded its review of Plaintiff’s claims because a final judgment on the merits had been rendered by a court of competent jurisdiction. Deaton, No. 4:23-CV-00415-O, 2024 WL 920082. The claims on the merits was not addressed.

Presently, Plaintiff’s Motion to Disburse attempts to once again challenge the validity of the Texas Final Arbitration Award of 2024 to get another bite at the apple, but this Court’s hands are tied. Since this Court did not actually adjudicate the underlying merits of the claims, this Court does not have the authority to apply the relitigation exception. Chick Kam Choo, 486 U.S. at 148 (“an essential prerequisite for applying the relitigation exception is that the claims or issues which the federal injunction insulates from litigation in state proceedings actually have been decided by the federal court”). As Atlantic Coast instructs lower courts, this element must be held to a “strict and narrow” standard. Id.; see generally Atl. Coast Line R. Co., 398 U.S. 281.

B. Enjoining Plaintiff Under the All Writs Act is Unwarranted.

Plaintiff’s actions do not “diminish[] the [C]ourt’s power to bring the litigation to its natural conclusion” because the question presently before the Rhode Island Superior Court was not litigated nor decided by this Court. Barton, 569 F.2d at 1358–59. JLF Defendants claim that Plaintiff, a vexatious litigant, brings the state court action to relitigate matters already decided.12 Additionally, they ask this Court to require Plaintiff to “withdraw his Motion to Disburse with prejudice” and to “prevent[] him from taking any position in any future litigation” that is contrary to the Texas Arbitration Award.13 Plaintiff’s actions do not disturb this Court’s judgment. Barton,
569 F.2d at 1358–59. Plaintiff’s Motion to Disburse calls into question the validity, perhaps, of the Final Arbitration Award entered by the Texas state court. However, it is the Rhode Island Superior Court’s duty to decide whether the claims before it have already been litigated such that res judiciata applies. Because the All Writs Act must be applied narrowly, we refuse to enjoin Plaintiff.

For Plaintiff to be deemed a vexatious litigant, the Fifth Circuit requires the JLF Defendants to proffer clear and convincing evidence that “every facet of [this] litigation was patently meritless” as well as evidence of Plaintiff’s “bad faith, improper motive, or reckless disregard of the duty owed to the court.” Bryant v. Mil. Dep’t of Mississippi, 597 F.3d 678, 694 (5th Cir. 2010) (quoting Procter & Gamble Co. v. Amway Corp., 280 F.3d 519, 525–26 (5th Cir. 2002) (emphasis in original)).

C. The Court Refuses to Order Parties to Arbitration.

Additionally, JLF Defendants ask this Court to order arbitration for any conceivable remaining disputes. Because this Court’s order of March 3, 2024 concluded that any claims or disputes that have been brought or could have been brought by Plaintiff during the Texas Arbitration were barred from litigation, the Court does not find it necessary to order parties to arbitrate. Once again, it is Rhode Island Superior Court’s responsibility to decide whether the case before them is barred by res judicata such that the claim must be dismissed, or that there are remaining claims that must be adjudicated such that arbitration may be warranted. Therefore, JLF Defendants’ Motion to Order Arbitration is DENIED.

It is hereby ORDERED that JLF Defendants’ Motion to Reopen this Matter and for Injunctive Relief, or Alternatively, Application for Arbitration (ECF No. 96) is DENIED.
