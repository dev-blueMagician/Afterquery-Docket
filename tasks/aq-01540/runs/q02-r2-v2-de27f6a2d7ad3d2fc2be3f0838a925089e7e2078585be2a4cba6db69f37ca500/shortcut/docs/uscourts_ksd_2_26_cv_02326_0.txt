Plaintiff Sethmar Transportation, LLC brings this diversity action against Defendants DUAL North America, Inc. (“DUAL”), Chaucer Syndicate 1084 of Lloyd’s (“Chaucer”), and Houston Casualty Company (“Houston”) (collectively, “Defendants”), for declaratory judgment and breach of contract. Before the Court is Defendants’ Motion to Transfer Venue Under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) (Doc. 13), DUAL’s Motion to Dismiss Pursuant to F.R.C.P. 12(b)(6) (Doc. 10), and DUAL’s Request for Hearing on Motion to Dismiss (Doc. 19). The motions are fully briefed, and the Court is prepared to rule. For the reasons explained below, the Court denies each motion.

II. Motion to Transfer Venue
Section 32 of the Policy contains a forum-selection clause that provides in relevant part:
Any suit, action or proceeding arising out of or based upon this policy shall be instituted in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York or the United States District Court in the district in which this policy was issued. Each party to this contract submits to the exclusive jurisdiction of such courts in any suit, action or proceeding and waives any objection to venue of any suit, action or proceeding in the said courts.11

III. DUAL’s Motion to Dismiss
DUAL individually moves to dismiss Plaintiff’s claims against it pursuant to Fed. R. Civ.
P. 12(b)(6), and it separately moved for a hearing on this motion. As a preliminary matter, after considering the parties’ briefs and the relevant law, the Court denies DUAL’s motion for hearing because it would not materially assist the Court in deciding this motion. Moreover, for the reasons below, the Court denies DUAL’s Motion to Dismiss.

To survive a motion to dismiss brought under Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6), a complaint must contain factual allegations that, assumed to be true, “raise a right to relief above the speculative level”39 and must include “enough facts to state a claim for relief that is plausible on its face.”40 Under this standard, “the complaint must give the court reason to believe that this plaintiff has a reasonable likelihood of mustering factual support for these claims.”41 The plausibility standard does not require a showing of probability that “a defendant has acted unlawfully,” but requires more than “a sheer possibility.”42 “[M]ere ‘labels and conclusions,’ and ‘a formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action’ will not suffice; a plaintiff must offer specific factual
